# Master Content — Containers Are Containers — Safe Storage by Function

## Big idea
A container is not a water-storage system because it can hold water. It becomes a usable system only when it protects the water **for the job that water must do**.

The right question is not:

> What barrel should I buy?

It is:

> What must this storage system do, what can contaminate or damage the water, how will I get the water back out, and what happens if the system fails?

## The five-question storage test

### 1. What is the water for?
Drinking/cooking, emergency household reserve, livestock, irrigation, postharvest produce contact, cleaning, or something else?

The same container can be acceptable for one job and unacceptable for another.

### 2. What did the container hold before?
For potable water, previous toxic-chemical service is a stop sign. CDC and WHO specifically warn against reusing containers that held pesticides, petroleum products and other non-food-grade liquids for drinking-water storage.

### 3. Can contamination get in?
Check the lid, opening, vent, spigot, animal/insect access, dirty hands, cups/dippers, nearby chemicals and the way the container is filled.

For potable water, a covered narrow opening plus dispensing that avoids hand/object contact is a repeated safe-storage principle across CDC and WHO guidance.

### 4. Can you clean, inspect and maintain it?
A giant tank that nobody can inspect or clean can become a liability. A small container that is easy to carry but constantly opened and dipped into can also fail.

### 5. What is the failure consequence?
If this water is for drinking, contamination can directly affect health. If it is for livestock, dirty water can reduce intake or affect animal health/performance. If it contacts fresh produce, contamination can move into the food system. If it is landscape irrigation, the consequence may be different.

**Requirements follow consequences.**

## See the system

```text
SOURCE / TREATMENT
        ↓
INTENDED USE
        ↓
CONTAINER HISTORY + MATERIAL + CONDITION
        ↓
LID / OPENING / VENT / DISPENSING
        ↓
LOCATION: light + heat + chemicals + animals
        ↓
CLEAN / INSPECT / ROTATE / TEST WHEN NEEDED
        ↓
USE THE WATER WITHOUT RECONTAMINATING THE SUPPLY
```

## Branch 1 — Drinking and cooking water
The strongest household controls are:

- safe/appropriately treated incoming water;
- clean, tightly closed storage;
- potable/food-contact-suitable container when applicable;
- small/narrow opening or sanitary tap/spigot;
- no prior toxic-chemical contents;
- cool/shaded placement away from gasoline, pesticides and similar chemicals;
- labeling and date tracking;
- cleaning/sanitizing;
- dispensing without putting hands, cups or dirty tools into the supply.

For emergency preparedness, CDC currently recommends storing at least 1 gallon per person per day for three days and aiming for two weeks when possible. CDC also recommends replacing self-filled emergency water every six months.

## Branch 2 — Livestock water
The goal changes from household potable storage to **reliable clean, fresh animal water**.

Design questions become:
- Is there enough capacity?
- Can animals contaminate the trough with manure/feed/dirt?
- Is algae building up?
- Can the tank be cleaned easily?
- Can it freeze, leak or run dry?
- Is the source quality known when concerns arise?

Do not use human emergency-jug rules as if they were automatically livestock standards. Research the animal and system when specific thresholds matter.

## Branch 3 — Irrigation and produce-contact water
"Irrigation water" is too broad to be one answer.

A container feeding ornamental plants is not the same risk as stored water sprayed directly onto edible produce or used in postharvest washing.

Ask:
- Does the water touch the edible part of the crop?
- Is it preharvest or postharvest?
- What is the source risk?
- Can contamination build up in the tank?
- What testing/risk assessment applies to this use?

## Branch 4 — Emergency reserve
Emergency storage is an inventory-and-reliability problem as much as a water problem.

A useful system considers:
- how many people/animals depend on it;
- how many days it must cover;
- whether one large tank creates a single point of failure;
- how water is accessed if power is out;
- rotation dates;
- cleaning and inspection;
- backup treatment if stored/source water becomes questionable.

## Traditional knowledge that still transfers
Across regions, people have used ceramic vessels, metal vessels, cisterns and covered containers because the storage functions are ancient.

Modern evidence does not prove that every old vessel was safe. It does show why several old design instincts still make sense:
- protect the opening;
- keep contamination out;
- separate clean water from dirty surroundings;
- make the vessel cleanable;
- avoid unnecessary handling;
- match storage to local conditions and actual use.

The lesson is not "old good, modern bad." The lesson is **understand why a design works before copying the form.**

## Use what you have — but pass the suitability gate first

### Potentially usable
An existing clean, intact container whose prior contents, material, closure, cleanability and environment fit the intended job.

### Conditional / non-potable only
A reusable vessel that can responsibly perform a lower-consequence function but cannot be verified as suitable for potable water.

### Worth buying
Purpose-made potable storage, a sanitary spigot, proper tank fittings, a protected lid, a stable support or a testing/monitoring component when it materially improves reliability or safety.

### Do not use for potable storage
Containers that previously held toxic chemicals, petroleum products, pesticides or other unsuitable materials.

## Practical activity — The Container Audit
Walk through every water container you already own or could reasonably access.

Record:
1. container type and size;
2. previous contents;
3. intended new use;
4. material/condition;
5. lid/opening;
6. dispensing method;
7. cleaning access;
8. sun/heat exposure;
9. nearby contamination sources;
10. result: **Suitable / Conditional / Non-potable only / Do not use**.

The point is not to buy something. The point is to stop guessing.

## Practical activity — Failure Before Failure
Pick one proposed storage setup and ask:
- What if the lid is left open?
- What if the spigot is dirty?
- What if a child dips a cup into it?
- What if the tank sits beside gasoline or pesticide?
- What if one animal contaminates the trough?
- What if the pump fails?
- What if sunlight heats the container all summer?
- What if the single giant tank cracks?

Then redesign the system to remove the highest-consequence failure first.

## Verification
A storage setup is not proven safe because the water looks clear.

Verification may include:
- inspecting the vessel and fittings;
- checking cleaning/rotation records;
- monitoring source and storage conditions;
- water-quality testing when a result changes the decision;
- laboratory testing for potable/agricultural risks when the consequence warrants it.

Testing refines the system. It does not prevent the learner from understanding storage functions first.

# TRANSFER THE PRINCIPLE
**Requirements follow the job, not the label on the object.**

A barrel, jug, tote, cistern or trough is only a form. First define the outcome, risks, required functions and failure consequence. Then decide whether the form fits.

Use the same thinking with food storage, fuel storage, inventory, emergency money, business capacity and family resources.

Where the analogy stops: chemical compatibility, microbiological contamination and potable-water safety are physical-system requirements and should not be treated as literal equivalents in money, business or relationships.
