# RRA-2026-0003 — Concept Foundation

**Working title:** Fact, Opinion & Bias — How to Tell What You're Actually Looking At

## Learning outcome

The learner can identify what kind of claim is being made, separate evidence from interpretation and preference, recognize common bias pressure in themselves and others, test a conclusion before treating it as fact, and make a more responsible decision about what to believe, repeat, share or act on.

## Central question

> **Am I looking at a fact, an interpretation, an opinion, a prediction, or a mixed claim — and what is influencing how I judge it?**

## Major principles

1. Claim type comes before agreement.
2. A factual claim is checkable; it is not automatically true because someone labels it a fact.
3. An opinion can be informed by evidence without becoming a factual claim.
4. Evidence quality and claim type are separate questions.
5. Bias can influence judgment without proving the final conclusion false.
6. Repetition is exposure, not independent corroboration.
7. Bias is easier to see in other people than in ourselves.
8. Intelligence alone is not a bias shield.
9. “Consider the opposite” and accuracy-focused pauses can improve some judgments.
10. A correction is stronger when it explains what the evidence supports instead.

## Historical / less-common knowledge angle

Francis Bacon's 1620 “Idols” framework documented the long-standing concern that the human mind can distort what it thinks it is merely observing. RRA can use this as a historical bridge, while clearly distinguishing historical philosophy from modern experimental psychology.

## Modern-use angle

Use the framework on:

- social-media posts and headlines;
- advertising and sales claims;
- family disagreements;
- school/work arguments;
- business decisions;
- rumors;
- political or cultural claims without making the lesson partisan;
- emotionally loaded personal decisions.

## Practical learner method

### SORT → SOURCE → STRESS TEST → DECIDE

**SORT** — What type of claim is this?  
**SOURCE** — What is the closest evidence and where did it actually come from?  
**STRESS TEST** — What would weaken this? What would the opposite predict? What am I personally motivated to believe?  
**DECIDE** — How confident should I be, and what action is justified by the evidence?

This is a working teaching mechanism for Product Opportunity Research to evaluate; it is not a locked public product name or tagline.

## Activity directions

- Claim Sorting: fact / interpretation / opinion / prediction / mixed.
- Evidence Ladder: rank support separately from confidence.
- Trace the Source: detect fake corroboration where many posts repeat one origin.
- Bias Mirror: identify pressure on “their side” and “my side.”
- Consider the Opposite: identify evidence that would weaken the learner's preferred conclusion.
- Decision Replay: compare what was known, assumed, predicted and later observed.
- Before You Share: accuracy-attention checklist.

## Visual needs for later evaluation

- repetition versus corroboration diagram;
- fact / inference / value layers in one real-world disagreement.

## Safety / professional boundary

This is educational critical-thinking material, not a clinical assessment of another person's cognition and not a license to diagnose motives or bias. Learners should not label disagreement as pathology.

# TRANSFER THE PRINCIPLE

The transferable skill is **separating observation, evidence, inference and preference before making a decision**.

It applies to relationships, business, money, leadership, media, school and everyday problem-solving. The comparison stops where a genuine value judgment cannot be settled by factual evidence alone.
