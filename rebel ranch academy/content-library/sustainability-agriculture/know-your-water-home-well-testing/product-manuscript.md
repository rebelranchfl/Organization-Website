# OPENING — DON'T START WITH THE KIT

A water test is useful only when it answers a question that changes a decision.

The weak sequence is:

`WORRY → BUY A BIG TEST → GET A PAGE OF NUMBERS → GUESS WHAT TO DO`

The stronger sequence is:

`UNDERSTAND THE SOURCE → NAME THE USE → IDENTIFY THE RISK OR EVENT → ASK THE RIGHT QUESTION → GET THE RIGHT EVIDENCE → READ THE RESULT IN CONTEXT → DECIDE WHAT JOB COMES NEXT → VERIFY`

That is the system this product builds with you.

You do not need every answer before you can learn how water testing works. Testing refines decisions. It is not permission to learn.

# 1 — WHAT WATER ARE WE TALKING ABOUT?

## Private well
If your household uses a private well, routine public-system monitoring is not happening on your behalf under the federal Safe Drinking Water Act. The well owner carries responsibility for checking the water.

## Public/community water
Public systems have a different monitoring structure. You can still use this product to understand a household concern, a treatment-performance question, or a sample/result, but do not pretend the responsibility structure is the same as a private well.

### Your action
Choose the source that matches the water you are evaluating.

**Your plan updates:** Source.

## Show me why
The approved evidence base supports owner responsibility for private wells and distinguishes private-well monitoring from public-system monitoring.

# 2 — WHAT DO YOU USE THIS WATER FOR?

One source can serve several jobs at once. Do not force yourself to choose only one.

Select every use that matters to your household:
- drinking/cooking;
- infant formula or young-child use;
- washing/fixtures/appliances;
- animals;
- irrigation/garden;
- emergency backup;
- checking whether treatment is working;
- another use you want remembered.

The product keeps these uses visible because the consequence of a bad assumption can differ by use.

### Important boundary
This product does not invent suitability limits for animal or irrigation use where the approved research does not contain them. It remembers those uses and tells you where use-specific evidence is still needed.

**Your plan updates:** Intended uses.

# 3 — WHY ARE YOU TESTING NOW?

Routine testing and event-triggered testing are different jobs.

## Routine / baseline
A baseline helps you know what “normal” looks like for this source over time.

EPA's general private-well guidance includes annual testing for total coliform bacteria, nitrate, total dissolved solids and pH, with additional testing when other contaminants are suspected. Florida currently recommends at least annual bacteria and nitrate testing for private wells and says most homeowners should test for lead every three years.

These are not one universal national rule. Florida guidance stays Florida-specific.

## Event or change
Testing may become more urgent or more targeted after:
- flooding;
- a well, pump, casing, storage or plumbing repair;
- a sudden taste, odor, color or clarity change;
- a known local spill or contamination issue;
- recurring gastrointestinal illness concerns;
- a new infant/pregnancy or other vulnerable household situation;
- a treatment-performance question;
- moving into a home with missing testing history.

### Your action
Select every reason that applies.

**Your plan updates:** Routine questions and event-triggered questions separately.

# 4 — WHAT LOCAL RISKS SHOULD YOU ASK ABOUT?

The point is not to memorize a giant contaminant list. The point is to connect a plausible contamination pathway to a useful question.

Approved Florida guidance identifies risk categories such as:
- septic systems;
- livestock areas;
- fertilizers/agriculture;
- leaking fuel tanks;
- pesticide use;
- landfills/improper hazardous-waste disposal;
- local geology or naturally occurring contaminants.

### Ask locally
Use your county/state health or environmental contact, a certified laboratory, or another appropriate local source to ask what is known to matter in your area.

### What you can do before results
You can:
- identify nearby risk categories;
- gather old well/testing records;
- identify recent floods/repairs/changes;
- contact local testing resources;
- decide what question needs answering first;
- prepare a testing plan.

### What should wait
Do not choose a treatment device for an unknown contaminant job merely because the water looks, smells or tastes unusual.

**Your plan updates:** Local-risk questions and unresolved unknowns.

# 5 — BUILD YOUR TESTING-QUESTION MAP

For each question, fill these five boxes:

1. **What are we trying to learn?**
2. **Why does it matter to this household/use?**
3. **What decision would the answer change?**
4. **What evidence route can answer it?**
5. **What should wait until the result is known?**

Example:

**Question:** Does this private-well water contain nitrate at a level that changes infant-formula use?

**Why it matters:** The approved evidence identifies infant nitrate risk as a higher-urgency situation.

**Evidence route:** Appropriate decision-grade laboratory testing.

**Decision changed:** Whether this water should be used for infant formula/food and what corrective next action is needed.

**Approved benchmark example:** EPA drinking-water MCL for nitrate is 10 mg/L; EPA's infant guidance says not to use private-well water above 10 mg/L nitrate for infant formula or food.

That example is supported. Do not generalize one threshold to unrelated analytes.

# 6 — CHOOSE THE EVIDENCE ROUTE

A testing route is a tool. Choose it by the job it must perform.

## County/state route
Useful when the exact service is verified and matches your question. Florida currently describes routine local testing as commonly around $20–$30 per sample. Availability and analytes vary by location.

## Certified local private laboratory
Useful when you need decision-grade drinking-water analysis and a local provider has the required analyte/method.

## Mail-to-lab service
May provide convenient logistics or broader panels. Broad is not automatically better. First ask whether the panel answers your actual question.

## Limited home screening
May be useful for learning or a limited screen, but an unverified consumer kit must not be presented as equivalent to a certified laboratory when a drinking-water health/safety decision depends on the number.

### Route comparator questions
- Does it test the thing I actually need to know?
- Is the result good enough for the consequence of the decision?
- What will it cost?
- How long will it take?
- What bottle/sample rules apply?
- Do I get an exact number or a broad screen?
- What will I still have to do afterward?

**Your plan updates:** Selected route and why.

# 7 — THE SAMPLE IS PART OF THE TEST

A laboratory cannot repair a sample that was collected or handled incorrectly.

Different analytes and labs can require different containers, timing, preservation and handling.

Do not memorize one universal collection ritual.

### Your action
Store the provider's actual instructions in your plan:
- container/bottle;
- sample location;
- whether treatment is included or bypassed when relevant;
- collection date/time;
- temperature/preservation instruction;
- delivery window;
- provider contact/reference.

Florida's bacteriological lab instructions are a good example of why details matter: official collection materials, avoiding contamination, cold transport and same-day delivery can matter for that service. Those instructions are not a universal rule for every analyte.

**Your plan updates:** Sample Journey.

# 8 — READ THE RESULT IN CONTEXT

A result is a measurement, not a complete diagnosis.

Record:
- test/analyte name;
- detected/not detected or numeric result;
- number;
- units;
- reporting/detection limit if supplied;
- sample location;
- before/after treatment status;
- benchmark/source being used;
- intended use affected;
- whether confirmation is needed.

## The four questions
1. What was measured?
2. In what units and at what sample point?
3. What benchmark or evidence are we comparing it with?
4. What decision changes now?

Do not strip a number away from its units, sample location or evidence source.

# 9 — WHAT JOB COMES NEXT?

Testing does not automatically mean “buy treatment.”

A result can lead to different jobs:

## Confirm / retest
Use when the consequence is important and confirmation is appropriate before a major corrective decision.

## Inspect the source/well/system
Some problems point to source, well, casing, plumbing, storage or other system correction rather than a treatment device.

## Define a treatment job
If a confirmed problem requires treatment, hand a **defined treatment job** to the parent Water product. Example form:

> “We need to address [identified problem] at [sample/use point] while preserving [selected uses], and we need a way to verify performance afterward.”

This testing product stops there. It does not duplicate the parent Water product's treatment-system architecture.

## Maintain / monitor
Some results support continued monitoring rather than immediate new equipment.

## Contact local public-health/environmental support
Use when the result, event, local contamination issue or household vulnerability needs local interpretation or action.

**Your plan updates:** Next job.

# 10 — VERIFY THE CORRECTION

Responsible Rebellion does not stop when the fix looks successful.

Use:

`UNDERSTAND → OBSERVE → TEST → COMPARE → IMPROVE → DECIDE`

For a treatment-performance question, preserve enough context to compare before and after responsibly:
- same defined question;
- appropriate sample locations;
- appropriate test method;
- timing/context recorded;
- result compared with the relevant evidence target.

**Permanent rule:** A normal-looking sample or successful-looking treatment is not proof. Test the result.

# 11 — YOUR LIVING WELL TEST PLAN

Your plan should now contain:

- routine baseline questions;
- event-triggered questions;
- local-risk questions;
- vulnerability/urgency notes;
- chosen test route;

- results with context;
- unresolved unknowns;

The plan should update when you change an earlier answer. You should not have to rebuild it from memory every year or after every event.

# 12 — DOES RESPONSIBLE REBELLION HOLD WATER?

## Activity A — Broad panel vs targeted question
Pick one real or simulated household question. Compare:
- a verified local targeted route;
- a broad mail-to-lab panel.

Compare:
- whether each answers the actual question;
- verified cost;
- extra analytes that may or may not change the decision;
- logistics;
- turnaround;
- what still must be interpreted afterward.

Do not choose the cheaper or broader route automatically. Choose the route that produces the evidence the decision requires.

## Activity B — Sensory assumption vs evidence
Start with a clear-looking or unusual-looking sample as an observation only.

Write what appearance makes you assume. Then compare that assumption with an appropriate test result or supplied case example.

Lesson:
**Appearance is information. It is not proof of safety.**

## Activity C — Plan changes after an event
Build a routine plan. Then simulate:
- flood;
- well repair;
- new infant in the household;
- local contamination notice.

Watch which questions change and which baseline records stay useful.

# TRANSFER THE PRINCIPLE

The deeper skill is:

> **Measure the condition that determines the decision before buying the solution.**

Try it elsewhere:
- soil: identify the actual soil problem before adding random amendments;
- business: find the bottleneck before buying software;
- money: measure the spending pattern before cutting random categories;
- repair: diagnose the failure before replacing parts.

## Where the comparison stops
A water lab result, business metric, soil test and repair measurement are not interchangeable forms of evidence. The transferable part is the diagnostic sequence: define the question, get evidence that answers it, then act.

# SOURCE DEPTH

The learner-facing product must provide optional source access to the approved source audit, including current EPA private-well guidance, Florida Department of Health private-well guidance, laboratory instructions and certified-lab resources.

Sources are optional learner depth, not a wall the learner must climb before using the product.
