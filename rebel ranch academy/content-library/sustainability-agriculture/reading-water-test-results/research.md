# Research Foundation — Reading Water Test Results Without the Confusion

Project: RRA-2026-0008
Research stage: Initial foundation
Source set status: Agent-vetted; owner overrides applied where present.

> Authority does not replace evidence. Proximity to the source, transparency, corroboration, and relevance matter more than institutional prestige.

> Teach transferable principles, not isolated facts.

## Research question
Once a household receives a private-well or home water test report, how should the learner read the result, compare it with the correct benchmark, understand what the test does and does not prove, decide what needs confirmation, and identify the next treatment/source/maintenance question without turning one number into a complete diagnosis?

## Research conclusion
The strongest RRA interpretation model is a layered decision sequence:

`WHAT WAS TESTED → WHAT DID THE LAB REPORT → IN WHAT UNITS → HOW LOW COULD THIS METHOD RELIABLY DETECT/QUANTIFY → WHAT BENCHMARK IS ACTUALLY RELEVANT → DOES THE SAMPLE CONTEXT MATCH THE QUESTION → WHAT DOES THE RESULT CHANGE → CONFIRM/INVESTIGATE/TREAT/MONITOR → RETEST/VERIFY`

The report is evidence. It is not the whole story.

A useful result has to be read together with:
- analyte or organism;
- numerical result or presence/absence call;
- units;
- qualifier such as non-detect/present;
- detection/reporting/quantitation limit when supplied;
- sample date and sample point;
- whether water was raw, treated or post-treatment;
- method/lab information;
- the correct health, regulatory, aesthetic or operational benchmark;
- intended water use;
- household vulnerability and local context;
- whether confirmation/retesting is appropriate.

This gives RRA a practical way to teach technical literacy without pretending the learner must become a laboratory scientist.

## 1. Start with the identity of the result
Before interpreting the number, identify **exactly what was tested**.

Water reports may contain:
- chemicals measured as concentrations;
- physical parameters such as pH or total dissolved solids;
- bacteria reported as presence/absence or counts;
- radiological measurements using different units;
- treatment-system indicators;
- aesthetic/operational measurements.

A learner should not compare unlike things simply because they all appear on the same report.

### Teaching rule
**Name the analyte first. Then interpret the value.**

A nitrate result is not interpreted like a total-coliform presence/absence result. pH is not interpreted like arsenic. A public-system action level is not automatically the same kind of benchmark as an MCL.

## 2. The result number means nothing without the unit
One of the easiest ways to misread a report is to compare numbers while ignoring units.

EPA technical guidance uses these common dilute-water equivalences:
- 1 mg/L = 1 part per million (ppm);
- 1 µg/L = 1 part per billion (ppb);
- 1 mg/L = 1,000 µg/L.

Example:
- 0.010 mg/L = 10 µg/L.

A report showing `10 µg/L` and a benchmark written as `0.010 mg/L` can represent the same concentration. A learner who compares only `10` to `0.010` can make a 1,000-fold interpretation error.

### Teaching implication
Every learner-facing result tool should force the question:

**Are the result and the benchmark in the same unit?**

If not, convert before comparing.

## 3. A benchmark has a type — not every line in a standards table means the same thing
EPA's drinking-water system uses several different kinds of values.

### MCLG — Maximum Contaminant Level Goal
EPA describes the MCLG as the maximum level at which no known or anticipated adverse effect on health would occur, with a margin of safety. It is a **non-enforceable public-health goal**.

### MCL — Maximum Contaminant Level
An MCL is an enforceable public-water-system standard. EPA sets MCLs as close to the MCLG as feasible while considering treatment technology and cost as well as health protection.

### Treatment Technique / Action Level
Some contaminants, including lead in public systems, are handled through treatment-technique/action-level structures rather than a simple universal household concentration limit. The learner should not casually convert a public-system compliance trigger into a claim that a private-well sample is legally compliant or noncompliant.

### Secondary drinking-water standards
EPA secondary standards address nuisance/aesthetic/technical effects such as taste, odor, staining, color, scale or corrosion. They do not carry the same federal health-regulatory meaning as National Primary Drinking Water Regulations.

### Teaching implication
The learner needs a **benchmark label**, not merely a benchmark number.

Use language such as:
- health-based goal;
- enforceable public-system limit;
- treatment/action trigger;
- secondary/aesthetic guideline;
- state/local private-well recommendation;
- laboratory reference range;
- manufacturer performance target.

Without the label, the number can mislead.

## 4. Public-system standards are useful evidence for private-well owners — but they are not the same legal system
EPA states that household private wells are not federally regulated under the Safe Drinking Water Act. The well owner is primarily responsible for household drinking-water safety.

At the same time, EPA's current private-well guidance explicitly points well owners to national primary and secondary drinking-water standards as useful references when interpreting test results.

These statements fit together:
- **private wells are not federally regulated like public water systems**;
- **public drinking-water standards can still provide useful health/quality reference points**;
- **state/local guidance and intended use still matter**.

### RRA boundary
Do not teach:
> `Below the federal MCL = your private well is legally safe.`

A more accurate teaching pattern is:
> `Compare the result with the current relevant health/quality benchmark, then check the private-well guidance that applies to your location and situation.`

## 5. Primary versus secondary exceedance changes the kind of problem you are solving
A result above a primary health standard can trigger a health/safety investigation and may justify confirmation, temporary source decisions, well/source inspection or contaminant-specific treatment.

A result above a secondary guideline may instead point primarily to:
- taste/odor;
- staining;
- scale;
- corrosion;
- fixture/equipment damage;
- customer/household acceptability.

These can still matter greatly. They simply answer a different question.

### Transferable lesson
Do not confuse:
- **harm**;
- **performance**;
- **comfort/aesthetics**;
- **regulatory compliance**.

The same distinction appears in machinery, food quality, business metrics and building systems.

## 6. Non-detect does not mean absolute zero
EPA quality-assurance guidance distinguishes a non-detect from a value of zero. When a chemical result is below an analytical detection limit, the true concentration is not known to be zero; it lies somewhere below the limit applicable to that method/data context.

Laboratories may use terms such as:
- MDL — method detection limit;
- MRL — minimum reporting level;
- RL — reporting limit;
- LOQ/LLOQ — limit/lower limit of quantitation;
- laboratory-specific qualifiers.

The exact term depends on the method and laboratory.

### Plain-language teaching
`ND`, `<`, or a similar qualifier often means:

**The laboratory did not reliably report the analyte above the method/reporting threshold shown — not that science proved there is literally none.**

### Why this matters
Suppose a health benchmark is lower than the lab's reporting limit. A non-detect may not be sensitive enough to demonstrate that the sample was below the health benchmark.

That is a powerful RRA teaching point: **method sensitivity must fit the decision.**

## 7. Detection and quantitation are different jobs
EPA defines the Method Detection Limit as a threshold related to distinguishing measured analyte from method-blank/background results with stated confidence. EPA drinking-water method documentation defines a Minimum Reporting Level as a concentration a laboratory can report as a quantified value under specified quality-control requirements.

The learner does not need to calculate these limits, but should understand the function:
- **detection question:** can the method distinguish something from background/noise?
- **quantitation/reporting question:** can the lab reliably report a numeric concentration at this level?

### Teaching implication
A result near the reporting/detection boundary deserves more care than a result far above it.

## 8. Bacteria reports work differently from ordinary chemical concentration reports
Total coliform and E. coli are important examples because many well-water reports use presence/absence language rather than a simple concentration-to-MCL comparison.

EPA explains that:
- total coliforms are a broad group and are commonly used as an indicator of possible system/source contamination;
- E. coli is more specifically associated with fecal contamination and carries a more direct health concern;
- public-system compliance procedures require follow-up when positive results occur.

Florida public-health laboratories test well-water samples for E. coli and coliforms, and Florida strongly recommends routine bacteria testing for private wells.

### Private-well teaching boundary
Do not copy the public-system regulatory compliance workflow as if it were a private-well legal requirement.

Do teach the evidence-based interpretation:
- a total-coliform positive result can signal a source, well, equipment, treatment or distribution integrity problem;
- E. coli presence is a more specific fecal-contamination warning;
- a positive result changes the urgency and follow-up path;
- sampling contamination is also possible, which is one reason collection method and confirmation matter.

## 9. Use concrete examples without pretending one example is the entire rulebook
### Nitrate
EPA's current primary standard for nitrate measured as nitrogen is 10 mg/L. Florida strongly recommends routine private-well nitrate testing and highlights vulnerability for infants and certain household situations.

Nitrate works well as a teaching example because:
- it is invisible;
- the unit matters;
- the threshold is numerical;
- household vulnerability can change urgency;
- a clear-looking sample can still carry a meaningful risk.

### Arsenic
EPA's current public-system MCL for arsenic is 0.010 mg/L (10 µg/L), while the MCLG is zero. This example demonstrates why MCL and MCLG are not interchangeable.

### Lead
EPA's current public-system lead framework uses a treatment technique and action level rather than a simple household MCL. This is a useful example of why a learner must identify the **benchmark type** instead of grabbing the first number found online.

### RRA rule
Worked examples are teaching tools. They do not authorize an all-contaminant result interpreter without analyte-specific current evidence.

## 10. Sample location changes the question the result answers
A result from:
- raw well water;
- kitchen tap;
- post-filter tap;
- storage tank;
- livestock line;
- irrigation point;
- a treatment-device outlet

can answer different questions.

For example, a post-treatment sample can test whether treatment is achieving the intended result, while a raw-water sample can help identify the source-water treatment job.

### Teaching implication
Every result interpretation should ask:

**Where was this sample taken, and what system components were upstream of it?**

This connects directly to the parent Water project without reopening it.

## 11. Date, event and trend can matter more than one isolated number
A single test is a snapshot.

RRA should teach learners to ask:
- Is this routine baseline testing?
- Did flooding, repair, nearby contamination or a sudden change trigger the sample?
- Is there an older result for comparison?
- Is the result stable, increasing, decreasing or newly detected?
- Was the treatment system serviced between samples?

Trend interpretation can reveal:
- deteriorating well integrity;
- treatment breakthrough;
- seasonal or event-related changes;
- maintenance needs;
- a one-off sampling anomaly requiring confirmation.

Research boundary: this cycle does not establish contaminant-specific statistical trend rules. It establishes the decision value of preserving context and prior results.

## 12. Confirmation is not the same as refusing to act
EPA private-well guidance advises retesting to confirm a contaminant's presence/concentration when a sample exceeds health standards and using public-health guidance for the next step.

This does **not** mean RRA should tell learners to ignore an elevated result until a second test comes back.

Responsible interpretation separates:
1. what the first result suggests;
2. whether a temporary protective action is sensible given the contaminant/risk;
3. what needs confirmation;
4. what source/well/treatment investigation follows.

### Evidence-first rule
Testing is a verification/refinement pathway, not a blanket permission gate on education.

RRA can teach what an elevated nitrate, coliform or arsenic result means in general while still saying the learner's exact corrective plan depends on the confirmed result, sample context and contaminant-specific evidence.

## 13. The result should produce a treatment job, not a shopping list
The practical endpoint of interpretation is not:

`RESULT → BUY FILTER`

It is:

`RESULT → IDENTIFY PROBLEM/FUNCTION → CHECK SOURCE/WELL/TREATMENT CONTEXT → CONFIRM WHEN NEEDED → DEFINE TREATMENT OR CORRECTION JOB → COMPARE OPTIONS → VERIFY RESULT`

Possible next jobs include:
- inspect/repair the well or source pathway;
- change source temporarily or permanently;
- remove a specific dissolved contaminant;
- disinfect/reduce microbes;
- correct corrosion/scale conditions;
- maintain or replace treatment media;
- investigate plumbing contribution;
- retest at another point in the system;
- monitor a non-health aesthetic condition;
- seek analyte-specific public-health/lab guidance.

This preserves `function before form` and protects the learner from product-first marketing.

## 14. A practical lab-report anatomy can be researched without choosing the final product format
The evidence supports a downstream visual that teaches the learner to circle/find:

1. **Sample ID / location** — what water did we actually sample?
2. **Sample date/time** — when?
3. **Analyte** — what was tested?
4. **Result** — number or presence/absence.
5. **Unit** — mg/L, µg/L, pCi/L, CFU/volume, pH, etc.
6. **Qualifier** — ND, <, present, absent or lab-specific code.
7. **Reporting/detection limit** — how low can this method/lab report reliably?
8. **Method** — how was it analyzed?
9. **Benchmark/reference** — what are we comparing it with, and what type of benchmark is it?
10. **Next decision** — confirm, inspect, treat, monitor, change source, or get analyte-specific help.

This is tangible-output research support, not premature Product Architecture approval.

## 16. What modernization improved — and what dependency it created
This topic does not need a romanticized historical section, but there is a useful modernization comparison.

### Earlier practical observation
People could observe:
- taste;
- odor;
- color;
- sediment;
- illness patterns;
- staining;
- scale;
- obvious source changes.

Those observations remain useful clues.

### Modern analytical improvement
Laboratory methods add the ability to:
- detect invisible contaminants;
- quantify concentration;
- compare with explicit benchmarks;
- use standardized methods and quality controls;
- track change over time.

### New dependency
Modern testing also creates dependence on:
- correct sampling;
- method sensitivity;
- laboratory access;
- reporting conventions;
- unit literacy;
- current benchmarks;
- interpretation.

### Transferable lesson
Modernization often reduces one uncertainty while creating a new knowledge dependency. The answer is not to reject the modern tool; it is to understand what the tool actually tells you.

# TRANSFER THE PRINCIPLE
The deeper principle is:

**A measurement only becomes useful when you know what was measured, how it was measured, what it is being compared with, and what decision the comparison changes.**

### Water
A nitrate result without units or a relevant benchmark is just a number.

### Money
`$4,000 in expenses` means little without period, revenue, category and target.

### Business
`30-minute cycle time` means little without the process definition, customer requirement and prior baseline.

### Health/fitness data
A wearable number needs measurement limits, context and a meaningful comparison before it should drive a decision.

### Soil
A nutrient number needs units, extraction/test method, crop context and target range.

### Equipment
A pressure reading needs gauge location, unit, system state and required operating range.

### Where the analogy stops
A water-laboratory result, financial metric, health measurement and equipment reading use different methods, risks and professional standards. The transferable principle is **measurement literacy**, not equivalence between the domains.

## Evidence boundaries / unresolved questions for later stages
- This foundation does not authorize a universal automated medical-style `safe/unsafe` label for every water analyte.
- Consumer test-kit result interpretation remains a separate evidence question because kit detection limits, accuracy and analyte coverage vary.
- PFAS, radionuclides, pesticides/VOCs and specialized contaminants require analyte-specific current evidence before detailed automated interpretation rules are built.
- Public-system compliance thresholds and procedures must not be misrepresented as private-well legal requirements.
- Laboratory qualifiers and reporting-limit terminology can vary; Product Design must either teach the lab's own definitions or build flexible fields rather than assume one universal code set.
- Exact current contaminant values used in learner-facing worked examples must be reverified before release.
- Product-market demand, comparable result interpreters, lab portals, pricing and product format remain Product Opportunity questions.
