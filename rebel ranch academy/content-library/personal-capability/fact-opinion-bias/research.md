# RRA-2026-0003 — Research Foundation

**Working title:** Fact, Opinion & Bias — How to Tell What You're Actually Looking At  
**Learning area:** Personal Capability  
**Research status:** COMPLETE — ready for owner research review

## Research question

How can RRA teach people to distinguish factual claims from opinions and interpretations, recognize the ways bias changes perception and judgment, and use practical methods to make better decisions without simply replacing one authority with another?

## Central finding

The strongest teaching model is **not** a two-box lesson where every statement is either “fact” or “opinion.” Real-life claims are often mixed.

A better RRA model separates at least five things:

1. **Factual claim** — a claim about what is or was the case that can, in principle, be checked against evidence.
2. **Interpretation / inference** — a conclusion drawn from observations or facts. It may be reasonable, weak, or wrong depending on the evidence and logic.
3. **Opinion / value judgment** — an evaluation, preference, ranking, moral judgment, or position. It can be informed by facts without becoming a factual claim itself.
4. **Prediction** — a claim about what will happen. It can be evidence-based but is not yet an observed fact.
5. **Mixed claim** — a sentence containing more than one of the above. Many arguments, headlines, advertisements and everyday disagreements are mixed claims.

This distinction matters because people often argue at the wrong level. Two people may agree on the factual record but disagree on interpretation or values. Or they may argue as if a factual question were only a matter of opinion.

## Fact is not the same thing as “something I strongly believe”

A factual claim is about reality and can be evaluated by evidence. Confidence does not determine truth. Agreement does not determine truth. Repetition does not determine truth. Authority does not determine truth.

A factual **claim** can be false. Calling a sentence factual means it is the kind of claim that can be checked; it does not mean it has already been proven true.

Example:

- “The meeting started at 9:03 a.m.” — factual claim; records, timestamps or witnesses may verify or challenge it.
- “The meeting started too early.” — evaluation/opinion; “too early” depends on a standard or preference.
- “Starting at 9:03 caused the poor turnout.” — causal interpretation; evidence beyond the start time is required.
- “Next week's meeting will be worse.” — prediction.

The learner's first question should be:

> **What kind of claim is this?**

Only then can the learner ask what kind of evidence would matter.

## Opinion is not automatically worthless or unsupported

Basic educational materials often teach “fact = supported” and “opinion = unsupported.” That is too crude.

A physician can hold a professional opinion based on extensive evidence. A mechanic can form an informed opinion about the likely cause of a problem. A family can hold a value judgment about what matters most. Those remain opinions, interpretations or judgments even when they are well informed.

Likewise, a factual claim can be stated with zero evidence or be outright false.

RRA should therefore teach two separate questions:

1. **What type of claim is it?**
2. **How strong is the support for it?**

Do not confuse claim category with evidence quality.

## Bias is not merely “having an opinion”

In cognitive research, biases are recurring tendencies that can systematically influence judgment. They often arise from useful mental shortcuts, prior beliefs, emotions, goals, memory, attention and social context.

Tversky and Kahneman documented how heuristics such as availability, representativeness and anchoring can produce predictable judgment errors. Kunda's motivated-reasoning framework describes how a desire to reach a particular conclusion can influence which beliefs and reasoning strategies people access and accept.

The practical lesson is not “your brain is broken.” The lesson is:

> **Your mind is an active interpreter, not a neutral camera.**

That means responsible judgment requires methods that help us check ourselves.

## The bias blind spot

Pronin, Lin and Ross found that people tend to see bias more readily in others than in themselves. This matters because “I know I'm objective” is weak protection against bias.

A powerful RRA rule follows:

> **If your bias-check only looks outward, it is incomplete.**

The learner should ask not only “What bias does that person have?” but also:

- What do I already want to be true?
- What group, identity, experience or outcome am I protecting?
- What evidence am I demanding from the other side that I did not demand from my own side?
- What fact would genuinely make me change my mind?

## Myside bias and intelligence

Research summarized by Stanovich, West and Toplak indicates that myside bias has little relationship to general intelligence across many studied paradigms.

That finding is educationally important. Being smart, educated, experienced or articulate does not make someone immune to one-sided evidence evaluation.

The risk can be worse in one practical sense: a capable reasoner may also be capable of producing sophisticated justifications for a preferred conclusion. Kunda's work emphasizes that desired conclusions are constrained by the ability to construct seemingly reasonable justifications — not necessarily by neutrality.

RRA should never teach “smart people are more biased.” The supported lesson is narrower:

> **Intelligence alone is not a reliable bias shield. Good reasoning requires deliberate habits.**

## Belief can interfere with logic

Evans, Barston and Pollard's syllogistic-reasoning experiments found substantial belief bias: people had more difficulty evaluating logical validity independently when conclusions were believable or unbelievable.

The everyday transfer is not that every disagreement is a logic puzzle. It is that **believability and validity are different questions**.

A claim can feel right because it fits what we already believe and still have weak reasoning. A claim can feel wrong and still be logically well supported.

## Repetition creates familiarity, and familiarity can feel like truth

The illusory truth effect is one of the clearest mechanisms for why repeated claims can become more believable.

Research shows that repeated statements often receive higher truth ratings than comparable new statements. Vellani and colleagues also found that repeated misinformation was more likely to be shared, with perceived accuracy helping explain the increase.

This creates a practical modern risk:

- a claim appears repeatedly in feeds;
- multiple people repeat the same wording;
- the claim becomes familiar;
- familiarity is experienced as “I've heard this everywhere”;
- “I've heard this everywhere” can be mistaken for independent corroboration.

RRA should teach:

> **Repetition is exposure. Corroboration is independent evidence. They are not the same thing.**

## Source quantity can be fake corroboration

Ten posts repeating one original claim are not ten independent sources.

When evaluating apparent agreement, trace backward:

- Are these sources independent?
- Are they all quoting the same study, press release, social-media post or unnamed source?
- Did anyone inspect the original evidence?
- Has the claim changed while being repeated?

This directly supports the Academy standard:

**Authority does not replace evidence. Proximity to the source, transparency, corroboration, and relevance matter more than institutional prestige.**

## Corrections do not always erase the first story

Research on the continued influence effect shows that misinformation can continue shaping reasoning after a correction. Kan and colleagues found that providing a plausible alternative explanation can reduce this effect more consistently than simply retracting misinformation.

Practical lesson:

When correcting a false claim, “that's wrong” may leave a hole in the person's mental model. A stronger correction often needs to explain:

1. what was wrong;
2. why it was wrong;
3. what the evidence supports instead;
4. how the mistaken belief may have formed.

## Debiasing: “be unbiased” is not enough

Lord, Lepper and Preston found that explicitly asking people to **consider the opposite** reduced some forms of biased social judgment more effectively than generic instructions to be fair and unbiased.

That gives RRA a practical method:

### THE OPPOSITE TEST

Before locking a conclusion, ask:

- If the opposite were true, what evidence would I expect to see?
- What is the strongest reasonable explanation someone on the other side could offer?
- What evidence would make my current conclusion weaker?
- Have I actually searched for that evidence?

This is not forced “both sides” thinking. Some claims have overwhelming evidence and weak counterclaims. The purpose is to test whether we have honestly examined alternatives.

## Accuracy requires attention

Pennycook and colleagues found that simply shifting people's attention toward accuracy improved the quality of information they intended to share. This suggests that misinformation is not always shared because people deeply believe it; attention may be focused on surprise, identity, emotion or social reaction instead of accuracy.

A practical RRA pause can therefore be simple:

### BEFORE YOU SHARE / REPEAT / DECIDE

1. What exactly is the claim?
2. Is this fact, interpretation, opinion, prediction or mixed?
3. What is the original evidence?
4. Is the source independent or repeating someone else?
5. What would disprove or weaken the claim?
6. Am I reacting to accuracy — or to how the claim makes me feel?
7. What happens if I am wrong?

## Historical pattern: bias did not begin with social media

Francis Bacon's *Novum Organum* (1620) described “Idols” that distort human understanding, including tendencies arising from human nature, individual background, language and accepted systems of thought.

Bacon's framework is not modern cognitive science and should not be presented as if he experimentally discovered today's named biases. Its educational value is historical:

> People have been wrestling for centuries with the problem that the human mind can mix its own assumptions into what it believes it is simply observing.

The technology changed. The human problem did not begin with the technology.

## What RRA should NOT teach

- “Everything is opinion.” False. Many claims are testable against reality.
- “If a person is biased, their conclusion must be false.” False. A biased process can still arrive at a true conclusion.
- “If a source has a conflict of interest, ignore it.” Too simplistic. A conflict is a reason to inspect methods/evidence more closely.
- “If experts disagree, nobody knows anything.” False. Disagreement has to be evaluated by the strength and scope of evidence.
- “Do your own research” means search until you find something you like. That is confirmation bias with extra steps.
- “Independent thinking” means rejecting conventional evidence. It does not.
- “Everyone gets their own facts.” They do not. People can disagree about evidence, interpretation and values, but reality does not change to fit preference.

## Confidence ladder

A useful learner-facing scale should separate certainty from evidence:

- **Observed / directly recorded** — strongest proximity for that event, but still check measurement/record quality.
- **Strongly corroborated factual claim** — multiple independent, relevant lines of evidence agree.
- **Reasonable inference** — evidence supports it, but alternatives remain possible.
- **Tentative hypothesis** — plausible and testable; not established.
- **Opinion / value judgment** — expresses preference, evaluation or principle; evidence may inform it but cannot fully decide the value question.
- **Unsupported assertion** — stated without adequate support.

The ladder should never imply that direct observation is infallible. Perception, memory and measurement can fail.

## Practical activity candidates

### 1. Claim Sorting
Give learners realistic statements and require two labels:
- claim type;
- evidence strength.

### 2. Trace the Source
Start with five posts that appear independent. Trace them backward to determine whether they originate from one source.

### 3. Bias Mirror
Learners identify likely bias pressures on both sides of a disagreement, including their own side.

### 4. Consider the Opposite
Write the strongest evidence that would make the learner's current belief less certain.

### 5. Decision Replay
Take a past decision and separate:
- what was known at the time;
- what was assumed;
- what was felt;
- what was predicted;
- what actually happened.

## TRANSFER THE PRINCIPLE

**Teach transferable principles, not isolated facts.**

The deeper principle is **separating reality, evidence, interpretation and preference before making a decision**.

### Personally
When you feel certain, ask what evidence created the certainty and what evidence would lower it.

### Family and relationships
Separate:
- “You came home at 8:25” (factual claim)
- “You don't care about this family” (interpretation about motive)
- “I need you home by 8” (expectation/value)

Many fights become clearer when the layers are separated.

### Business
Separate:
- sales are down 12% (measured fact if the records are accurate)
- customers hate the new price (hypothesis/interpretation)
- we should lower the price (recommendation/opinion)

Do not solve an interpretation as though it were measured fact.

### Money
Separate account balances and rates from predictions about what markets, prices or income will do next.

### Leadership
Do not confuse “everyone is saying it” with independent corroboration. Trace the source and identify the actual evidence before changing policy or direction.

### Where the analogy stops
Not every value disagreement can be settled by more evidence. Facts can clarify consequences, but moral priorities, preferences and goals may remain genuine value judgments.

## Research confidence

**High confidence:** systematic bias exists; myside/bias-blind-spot effects; repetition/illusory-truth effects; accuracy-attention and consider-the-opposite interventions can improve some judgments.

**Moderate confidence / context-dependent:** exact effectiveness of any debiasing exercise in everyday settings; transfer from controlled tasks to all real-world disputes.

**Unresolved but non-blocking:** best age-specific packaging and whether the strongest market opportunity is a general critical-thinking product, family/homeschool resource, or media-literacy module. Those are Product Opportunity Research questions, not evidence gaps blocking Research Review.
