# Fact, Opinion & Bias — Research Synthesis

**Project:** RRA-2026-0003  
**Stage:** Research Foundation  
**Audience:** Teens, adults, families, homeschool learners, owners/leaders

## The problem

A lot of arguments are not really arguments about the same thing.

One person is talking about an event. Another is talking about what they think the event means. A third is talking about what should happen next. Then everyone uses the word “fact” for whichever layer they feel most strongly about.

That creates confusion because strength of feeling and strength of evidence are not the same thing.

The first skill is not deciding whether you agree.

The first skill is deciding **what kind of statement you are looking at.**

## Five useful claim types

### 1. Factual claim

A factual claim says something about reality that can, in principle, be checked.

“The store closed at 6:00 p.m.”

That may be true or false. The statement is factual in type because records, signs, timestamps or observation can verify it.

### 2. Interpretation or inference

An interpretation explains what facts might mean.

“The store closed early because business was slow.”

That might be reasonable. It might also be wrong. A closing time does not prove motive or cause.

### 3. Opinion or value judgment

An opinion evaluates, ranks, prefers or judges.

“The store should stay open later.”

Evidence can inform the opinion. Sales data, staffing costs and customer demand matter. But “should” still depends partly on goals and values.

### 4. Prediction

A prediction says what is expected to happen.

“If the store closes at 6:00 again, sales will drop.”

The prediction can be evidence-based, but the future result has not happened yet.

### 5. Mixed claim

Most real arguments contain more than one layer.

“The store is losing customers because management does not care.”

This contains an empirical claim, a causal inference and a judgment about motive.

Before arguing, split the sentence apart.

## One sentence can hide three different disagreements

Consider:

“You never listen to me.”

Possible layers:

- observed events: several conversations were interrupted;
- interpretation: interruption means the other person was not listening;
- value: attentive listening is expected in the relationship;
- emotional experience: the speaker feels ignored.

Calling the whole sentence “fact” or “opinion” does not solve anything. Breaking the statement into layers gives people something they can actually examine.

## Facts need evidence; labels do not create facts

Calling something “a fact” does not make it one.

A factual claim becomes well supported when the available evidence actually matches the claim.

Useful questions:

- What is the exact claim?
- What evidence could verify it?
- How close is the source to the event/data?
- Is the source repeating somebody else?
- Are multiple sources truly independent?
- What evidence challenges the claim?
- What is still unknown?

**Authority does not replace evidence. Proximity to the source, transparency, corroboration, and relevance matter more than institutional prestige.**

## Opinions are not automatically bad

An informed opinion can be extremely valuable.

A mechanic's judgment about a strange noise can be based on thousands of hours of experience. A parent can make a value judgment about what is acceptable in their home. A business owner can judge that one customer experience matters more than short-term efficiency.

The mistake is not having an opinion.

The mistake is presenting a judgment, interpretation or preference as though reality itself has already proven it.

## Bias is part of the reasoning problem

The mind is not a video camera.

People notice some things more than others. Memory is selective. Prior beliefs affect what feels plausible. Desired outcomes can affect which explanations we accept. Familiar claims can feel more believable simply because they are familiar.

Research has documented systematic patterns in judgment including heuristics, motivated reasoning, myside bias, bias blind spots, belief bias and the illusory truth effect.

The goal is not to memorize a giant list of bias names.

The stronger skill is learning to recognize **where bias can enter the process.**

## Where bias enters

A useful chain is:

**EVENT / CLAIM → ATTENTION → MEMORY → INTERPRETATION → EVIDENCE SEARCH → CONFIDENCE → DECISION**

Bias can influence every middle step.

You can:

- notice evidence that supports your expectation;
- ignore evidence that feels inconvenient;
- remember examples that are emotionally vivid;
- search only for confirming material;
- demand stronger proof from people you disagree with;
- accept weaker proof from people you already trust;
- confuse familiarity with verification;
- defend a conclusion because changing it threatens identity, pride or belonging.

## The bias blind spot

One of the most useful findings for everyday life is that people commonly see bias more easily in other people than in themselves.

That means “they are biased” is not enough.

A serious bias check has two directions:

### Look outward

What incentives, assumptions, identity, framing or evidence limits may affect the other source?

### Look inward

What do I want to be true?

What do I already believe?

What would embarrass me if I had to admit I was wrong?

Would I accept this exact evidence if it supported the opposite conclusion?

## Repetition is not corroboration

A claim can appear to have massive support because it is repeated everywhere.

But if 50 posts all trace back to one original source, you do not have 50 independent confirmations.

You have one source repeated 50 times.

Research on the illusory truth effect shows why this matters. Familiarity itself can increase perceived truth.

So RRA should teach:

> **Count independent evidence, not echoes.**

## Being smart does not make you bias-proof

Bias is not a problem reserved for unintelligent people.

Myside-bias research shows weak relationships between many bias measures and general intelligence. Motivated reasoning also helps explain how people can construct sophisticated justifications for conclusions they prefer.

The useful lesson is not “educated people cannot be trusted.”

The lesson is:

> **Reasoning skill needs a checking process.**

A person who can argue well can still argue well for the wrong reason.

## The strongest practical correction: make the mind do something different

“Try not to be biased” is vague.

Research gives more concrete methods.

### Consider the opposite

Ask what evidence would exist if your current conclusion were wrong.

### Shift attention to accuracy

Before sharing or acting, stop and judge the claim's accuracy rather than its emotional, social or entertainment value.

### Replace, do not merely retract

When correcting misinformation, explain the supported alternative. Removing one explanation without replacing it can leave the original story influencing later reasoning.

## The RRA method: SORT → SOURCE → STRESS TEST → DECIDE

### SORT

What kind of claim is this?

- factual claim;
- interpretation/inference;
- opinion/value judgment;
- prediction;
- mixed claim.

### SOURCE

What is the closest available evidence?

Trace the claim backward. Separate independent sources from copies.

### STRESS TEST

What would weaken the conclusion?

What would the opposite predict?

Would I accept the same quality of evidence if it pointed the other way?

### DECIDE

How confident should I be?

What action is justified if the conclusion is only tentative?

What is the cost of being wrong?

## Confidence should match evidence

Instead of “true / false / I don't know,” learners need more useful confidence levels.

- **Strongly corroborated:** multiple relevant independent lines of evidence agree.
- **Supported:** evidence points in one direction but meaningful uncertainty remains.
- **Reasonable inference:** plausible explanation supported by some evidence; alternatives remain.
- **Tentative:** possible but not adequately established.
- **Value judgment:** evidence can inform it, but evidence alone does not settle the value question.
- **Unsupported assertion:** no adequate support has been shown.

Responsible thinking does not require pretending uncertainty is weakness.

Sometimes “I have enough evidence to lean this way, but not enough to call it settled” is the most accurate answer.

## Historical pattern: Bacon's “Idols”

In 1620 Francis Bacon described recurring ways the human understanding can distort what it thinks it sees. His categories included distortions common to human nature, individual background, language and inherited systems of thought.

He was not doing modern psychology. His framework is historically valuable because it shows the problem is older than television, the internet or social media.

Humans have long needed methods to stop their own minds from becoming the unexamined standard of reality.

## Practical examples

### Family

“Dad was 20 minutes late” — factual claim if timing is supported.

“Dad doesn't respect us” — interpretation of motive.

“Being late without calling is unacceptable” — value judgment.

“Dad will always do this” — prediction/generalization.

Different layers need different conversations.

### Business

“Sales fell from $18,400 to $15,900” — factual claim if records are accurate.

“Customers think our prices are too high” — hypothesis unless measured.

“We need to cut prices” — recommendation.

The business should not spend money solving a hypothesis before checking it.

### Social media

“Three local schools closed today” — factual claim that can be verified.

“The district is falling apart” — interpretation/value-loaded judgment.

“This proves every school will close next month” — unsupported prediction unless additional evidence exists.

## Activity: two labels, not one

For each statement, learners must assign:

**Label 1: Claim type**  
**Label 2: Evidence strength**

This prevents the common mistake of assuming “fact” means “proven” and “opinion” means “worthless.”

## Activity: Trace the Echo

Give learners five posts that all repeat the same claim.

Their job is to trace each one backward until they find the original evidence.

Then ask:

- How many independent sources were there really?
- Did any post add new evidence?
- Did wording become stronger as it spread?
- Did repetition make the claim feel established before verification?

## Activity: Bias Mirror

Choose a disagreement that matters but does not create a safety risk.

Write two columns:

**Bias pressure I can see on the other side**  
**Bias pressure that could affect my side**

The exercise is incomplete until the learner fills in both columns.

If the answer to “what would change my mind?” is “nothing,” the learner is no longer describing an evidence-responsive conclusion.

## TRANSFER THE PRINCIPLE

**Teach transferable principles, not isolated facts.**

The deeper method is:

> **Separate what happened from what you think it means, separate what you know from what you assume, and make confidence earn its way upward through evidence.**

### Relationships

Separating event, interpretation and expectation reduces arguments about motives.

### Money

Separate account facts from forecasts, hopes and fear.

### Business

Measure the problem before solving the story about the problem.

### Leadership

Trace repeated claims to their origin before changing direction.

### Learning

Do not ask only “Who said this?” Ask “What evidence would let me verify it?”

### Where the transfer stops

Facts cannot completely settle genuine value questions. Evidence can show likely consequences, but people may still disagree about priorities, morality, preferences and acceptable tradeoffs.

## Research boundary

This research foundation supports teaching critical-thinking habits. It does not support diagnosing another person as biased, manipulative or irrational based only on disagreement.

The best RRA outcome is a learner who becomes harder to fool **and** harder to make overconfident.
