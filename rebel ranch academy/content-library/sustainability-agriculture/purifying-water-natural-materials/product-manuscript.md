## Product promise

This product does not start by asking which filter you should buy. It starts by asking what you are actually trying to accomplish.

The goal is to help a learner move from a real water situation to a responsible system plan by understanding source, intended use, what is known, what remains unknown, what needs to be tested, which treatment jobs are actually required, what resources are already available, what can safely substitute, what is worth buying, how the system should be verified, and how it will be maintained.

The core Rebel Ranch Academy rule is simple:

> **Teach function before form.**

A filter, UV unit, bucket, pump, valve, storage tank, or test kit is a form. The learner first needs to understand the job that must be done.

# Module 1 — Stop Shopping. Define the Problem.

A common water mistake happens before anyone buys anything: the learner begins with a product instead of the problem.

“What filter should I buy?” sounds like a practical question, but it skips the most important step. A filter can only be judged against a treatment job. If the learner does not know the source, intended use, actual water condition, or contaminant concern, comparing products may create more confusion rather than less.

Start here:

1. What water source are you using?
2. What do you want to use the water for?
3. What do you actually know about the water?
4. What are you assuming?
5. What still needs to be tested or verified?
6. What would count as a successful result?

## Clear is not the same as safe

Visible dirt is useful information, but appearance alone cannot establish drinking-water safety. Water can look clear and still contain hazards that cannot be seen. Water can also look unpleasant because of sediment or minerals without that observation alone telling you the full health-risk picture.

The learner's job is therefore not to “make the water look good.” The job is to identify which functions the system must perform.

## Think Like a Rebel

Do not ask, “What product does everyone use?”

Ask:

> **What job needs to be done, under what conditions, and how will I know it worked?**

### Learner action — Define the goal

Complete:

- My source is: ______
- My intended use is: ______
- What I know: ______
- What I am assuming: ______
- What I still need to learn/test: ______
- Success would mean: ______

# Module 2 — Know Your Source

Different sources create different starting conditions. A municipal tap, private well, rainwater tank, stored emergency water, and pond are not the same problem wearing different labels.

## Municipal water

The learner often begins with existing treatment and public water-quality information. The next question may involve a specific concern, household plumbing, taste/odor preference, or a point-of-use treatment goal.

## Private well

The household is responsible for understanding and managing its own private supply. When water quality is unknown, the correct first step may be testing rather than treatment shopping.

## Rainwater

Rainwater must be evaluated by intended use, collection environment, storage, debris management, contamination pathways, and any treatment or verification needed for that use. Human potable use requires a different standard than irrigation or some animal uses.

## Pond, lake, creek, or other surface source

Surface water deserves a separate high-caution path. Microbiological hazards may be important, but chemical or toxin concerns can also change whether common emergency treatment methods are sufficient.

## Stored water

Storage is part of the system. Container suitability, cleanliness, closure, downstream contamination, time, and intended use matter.

### Learner action — Build your source profile

Record:

- source type;
- location/context;
- known treatment already occurring;
- known testing/report information;
- visible or sensory observations;
- recent changes/events;
- intended uses.

# Module 3 — What Do You Know? What Don't You Know?

This is where the learner separates evidence from assumption.

Create three columns:

### KNOWN
Measured, documented, directly observed, or supported by a current source record.

### ASSUMED
Something believed because of appearance, habit, marketing, past experience, or another person's statement.

### UNKNOWN
Information that would materially change the treatment decision.

An unknown is not a failure. It is a direction.

If the unknown could change what system is appropriate, the next job is to test, inspect, research, or obtain the relevant information.

## Activity — Dirty Is Visible. Danger Isn't.

Use the approved Academy investigation to compare what a learner can observe with what an observation cannot prove. The lesson is not that visible dirt is irrelevant. The lesson is that **observation has a limit**.

### Transfer the principle

The same problem appears in business, money, health, tools, and relationships: visible symptoms are not always the root problem. Diagnose before prescribing.

# Module 4 — What Should You Test?

Testing should answer a decision question.

## SCREEN IT

A low-cost screening method may be useful when the learner needs preliminary information or a simple parameter check. Screening is not automatically equivalent to laboratory analysis.

## TARGET IT

If a specific concern exists, choose a test that actually addresses that concern.

## KNOW IT

When drinking-water decisions depend on higher confidence, when the water source is unknown, or when the stakes are high, certified/laboratory testing may be the responsible path.

## Private-well rule

The product must surface the approved current private-well testing guidance from the research foundation and make clear that treatment selection follows evidence rather than guesswork.

### Learner action — Testing decision

Complete:

- What decision am I trying to make?
- What information would change that decision?
- Is screening enough for this decision?
- Is a targeted test needed?
- Is laboratory/certified testing needed?
- What result will I use to choose the next treatment job?

# Module 5 — Treatment Jobs, Not Product Names

A water system can contain several different jobs. Not every system needs every job.

## PROTECT

Reduce contamination before it enters the treatment chain when possible.

## SETTLE / SEPARATE

Allow larger solids to settle or remove larger debris.

## SCREEN / PREFILTER

Remove larger suspended material before later treatment steps.

## FILTER PARTICLES

Use an appropriate medium or device to reduce suspended particles within its performance limits.

## ADSORB SELECTED COMPOUNDS

Use appropriate carbon or other adsorption media when the identified treatment job matches the media's supported performance.

## REMOVE SPECIFIC DISSOLVED CONTAMINANTS

Use contaminant-specific treatment such as reverse osmosis, ion exchange, or specialized media only when the problem and performance evidence match.

## DISINFECT / INACTIVATE MICROORGANISMS

This is a separate job from particle filtration or chemical removal.

### Where UV belongs

UV belongs here: **microbiological disinfection/inactivation**.

UV is not a universal purifier. It does not remove sediment, dissolved chemicals, dissolved metals, or salts simply because water passes through a UV reactor.

For unknown private-well water, the path remains:

`TEST → IDENTIFY THE PROBLEM → ASSIGN THE TREATMENT JOB → SELECT A VALIDATED METHOD → VERIFY`

### UV needs suitable water entering the reactor

Particles and turbidity can interfere with UV exposure. Pretreatment may therefore be a required function before UV, depending on the tested water and the selected system's requirements.

### Class A and Class B are not interchangeable

The approved UV research requires the learner to check the actual NSF/ANSI 55 class and claim.

- **Class A** is associated with systems intended for contaminated water and microorganism inactivation claims.
- **Class B** is for reduction of non-disease-causing bacteria in water already disinfected.

A label that says only “NSF/ANSI 55” is incomplete for a buying decision. The learner also needs the class, model/configuration, and certified flow.

### Flow is part of the treatment condition

A validated UV device is not assumed to perform identically at any flow rate. The learner must respect the certified/model operating conditions.

### POU versus POE

- **Point of use (POU):** treats water at one delivery location or tap.
- **Point of entry (POE):** treats most or all water entering the home/system.

The correct choice depends on the identified microbial need, required flow, plumbing architecture, other treatment jobs, power, maintenance, and where untreated water would remain.

### UV does not protect forever downstream

UV does not leave a residual disinfectant in downstream plumbing or storage. The system still needs suitable storage/distribution hygiene and protection against recontamination.

### Maintenance is part of treatment

Power, optical cleanliness, lamp/LED output, sensors/alarms where present, flow conditions, and exact manufacturer service requirements are part of whether the system can continue to perform as intended.

Do not invent a universal lamp replacement or sleeve-cleaning interval. Follow the current instructions for the exact certified model.

### Potable-water DIY boundary

The learner may understand and build the surrounding system logic: testing, pretreatment, storage, gravity/pumps, plumbing layout, backup planning, and maintenance logic.

For safety-critical potable-water microbial disinfection, use a validated/certified UV reactor appropriate to the identified treatment job. Do not teach a homemade UV reactor as if a generic lamp or LED were equivalent to validated pathogen treatment.

### Other forms that can perform microbial-disinfection functions

Depending on the scenario and approved conditions, the learner may compare:

- boiling;
- chlorine-based disinfection;
- solar disinfection;
- validated UV.

The correct question is not “Which one is best?” It is:

> Which method can perform the required disinfection function under these conditions, with these resources, at this scale, and with an acceptable verification path?

## STORE

Hold water in a container suitable for the intended use.

## MOVE

Transfer water using gravity, tubing/pipe, pump, or another suitable method.

## CONTROL

Start, stop, regulate, or automate flow using appropriate valves, floats, switches, or controls.

## VERIFY

Use an appropriate test, inspection, indicator, specification, or retest to confirm the claimed result.

## MAINTAIN

Keep the system capable of performing over time.

# Module 6 — Function Before Form

A named object is one possible form of a function.

- jug / bucket / drum / tank → STORE
- hose / tubing / pipe → MOVE
- gravity / pump → CREATE FLOW OR PRESSURE
- valve / float → CONTROL
- test / laboratory analysis → VERIFY OR CHARACTERIZE
- activated carbon → ADSORB within supported claims
- UV reactor → DISINFECT MICROORGANISMS within validated claims

The learner should learn to redraw the problem as a system of jobs instead of a shopping list.

### Exercise — Remove the brand names

Take your current idea and replace each named product with its function.

Then ask:

1. Is that function actually required?
2. What performance is required?
3. What do I already have that can perform it?
4. What can safely substitute?
5. Where does substitution become unsafe or materially inferior?

# Module 7 — Use What You Have / Know When Not To

Resourcefulness is part of RRA. So are limits.

## USE WHAT YOU HAVE

Use an existing resource when it safely performs the required job.

## REUSE / REPURPOSE

Reuse only after suitability is established. Prior contents, material compatibility, cleanliness, structural condition, pressure, temperature, and intended use matter.

## SOURCE LOCALLY

Local availability can reduce cost and dependency, but local does not automatically mean suitable.

## WORTH BUYING

Purchase is justified when tested performance, accuracy, safety, durability, or reliability matters.

Examples may include:
- appropriate laboratory testing;
- contaminant-specific certified treatment components;
- a validated potable-water UV reactor when microbial disinfection is actually needed;
- suitable food-safe storage when available containers are inappropriate;
- reliable pumps/valves/controls where failure matters.

## OPTIONAL UPGRADE

Automation, larger capacity, monitoring, redundancy, easier cleaning, and convenience may improve the base system without being necessary to perform the essential function.

## DO NOT SUBSTITUTE

Do not substitute look-alike forms when the underlying performance cannot be established.

A generic UV light source is not automatically a validated microbial-disinfection reactor. A demonstration sand/charcoal filter is not automatically potable-water treatment. A container that holds liquid is not automatically suitable for drinking-water storage.

# Module 8 — Build the Minimum Responsible System

Now convert the required jobs into a sequence.

Use this structure:

`SOURCE → WHAT WE KNOW → WHAT MUST BE TESTED → REQUIRED FUNCTIONS → RESOURCES AVAILABLE → SAFE FORMS/SUBSTITUTIONS → WHAT MUST BE BOUGHT → VERIFY → MAINTAIN`

The simplest responsible system is not necessarily the cheapest system. “Simple” means it performs the required functions without adding unnecessary complexity.

### Learner build sheet

For each function, record:

- function;
- required performance;
- current resource/form;
- safe substitute options;
- purchase required?;
- failure mode;
- verification method;
- maintenance requirement.

# Module 9 — Scale It

The storage function is still storage whether the form is a one-gallon jug, five-gallon bucket, 55-gallon drum, or larger tank.

But scale changes the engineering problem.

The learner must account for:
- total weight;
- structural support;
- outlet height;
- gravity/head;
- flow demand;
- fittings;
- overflow;
- material suitability;
- cleaning access;
- stagnation and maintenance;
- portability versus fixed installation.

The principle is:

> **The function may stay the same while the constraints change.**

---

# Module 10 — Automate It

Automation should remove repeated labor without hiding the system from the learner.

A farm/chicken watering example can be decomposed as:

`SOURCE → STORAGE → CREATE FLOW → MOVE WATER → CONTROL DELIVERY → VERIFY ACCESS → CLEAN / MAINTAIN`

Possible forms include an elevated drum, gravity, pump, tubing, nipples/cups, float valves, or other controls depending on the actual setup.

Do not automatically transfer human potable-water requirements into animal distribution, or vice versa. Intended use matters.

### Automation questions

- What task repeats?
- What senses or controls the condition?
- What happens if the control fails?
- What backup keeps animals supplied?
- How will the learner inspect for leaks, blockage, contamination, or empty storage?

---

# Module 11 — Verify, Maintain, Retest

A system is not finished when water starts flowing.

Verification must match the claim.

If the claim is particle reduction, the evidence may involve a different method than if the claim is microbial safety or a specific dissolved contaminant.

Create a maintenance plan that records:

- what needs cleaning;
- what needs replacement;
- what needs inspection;
- what depends on power;
- what has a model-specific service schedule;
- what alarms/indicators must be checked;
- when retesting is triggered;
- what happens during failure;
- what backup source or method exists.

For UV, follow the exact certified model's current instructions for service intervals, lamp/LED requirements, optical cleaning, alarms, replacement parts, and operating conditions.

# Module 12 — Water Through Time & Place

Water knowledge developed differently because environments were different.

People worked with the source, fuel, materials, climate, settlement density, tools, and knowledge available to them. Some older methods performed useful treatment functions. Others had limits or risks that were not understood at the time.

The purpose of historical learning is not nostalgia. It is pattern recognition.

The approved research preserves examples involving natural filtration roles, Moringa, copper-vessel evidence, source protection, and differing global practices.

## Modernization tradeoff

Modern water systems improved repeatability, scale, monitoring, convenience, and many safety outcomes. They also allowed ordinary users to outsource more understanding of treatment, storage, source protection, flow, maintenance, and verification.

RRA's goal is not to reject modern systems. It is to restore the learner's ability to understand what the system is doing and make informed decisions about it.

# Module 13 — Match the Claim to the Evidence

Not every observed effect supports the conclusion someone wants to draw from it.

Use the Academy claim ladder:

`OBSERVATION → MECHANISM → PERSISTENCE → REPLICATION → BIOLOGICAL EFFECT → HEALTH EFFECT → RECOMMENDATION`

The approved acoustic/frequency and lunar-human material belongs here because it teaches evidence literacy.

A visible physical effect in water does not automatically prove permanent restructuring, biological benefit, or health benefit. A study showing an association does not automatically establish a reliable general effect without considering replication, method, competing findings, and publication bias.

The lesson is broader than water:

> **Match the strength of the conclusion to the strength and type of evidence.**

# Module 14 — Think Like a Rebel: Transfer the Principle

The water project is a case study in a reusable way of thinking.

Use the same method on another problem:

1. Define the real goal.
2. Separate facts from assumptions.
3. Identify what you still need to know.
4. Break the problem into functions.
5. Inventory what you already have.
6. Find safe alternatives that perform the same function.
7. Identify where buying is justified.
8. Build the simplest responsible system.
9. Identify upgrades and automation.
10. Name the tradeoffs.
11. Verify the result.

Possible transfer problems:
- irrigation;
- animal housing;
- household storage;
- emergency planning;
- business software selection;
- workflow design;
- budgeting and resource allocation.

# TRANSFER THE PRINCIPLE

A water system is useful because it teaches more than water.

The transferable principle is:

> **Understand the job before choosing the form. Use what you have when it is genuinely suitable. Buy where performance or safety justifies it. Verify the result.**

That is responsible independence—not guessing, not buying blindly, and not rejecting useful modern tools simply because they are modern.

## Safety stop rules

The tool must stop and route the learner to testing, research, a validated product requirement, or professional/laboratory help when a critical unknown materially changes the safe recommendation.

Examples include:
- unknown source/quality with human drinking intended;
- suspected contamination that has not been characterized;
- surface water with possible chemical/toxin concern;
- proposed unsafe storage/material substitution;
- microbial-disinfection request where the learner proposes an unvalidated homemade UV reactor;
- use of a treatment technology outside its supported/validated claim.
