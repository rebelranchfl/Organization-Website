1. Start With the Source

Different water sources create different first questions. Intended use changes the decision again.

Municipal

Start with utility/source information and the specific concern. Ask whether the need is taste, particles, a documented contaminant, or another treatment job.

Private Well

Testing comes first when the condition is unknown. Source condition, local risks and intended use drive the next step.

Rainwater

Ask how it was collected, stored and maintained—and what the water will be used for.

Stored Water

Ask what entered storage, how long it has been stored, container condition, distribution hygiene and intended use.

Surface Water

High-caution path. Unknown microbial, chemical and toxin hazards cannot be assumed away by ordinary filtration or one disinfection step.

SOURCE → INTENDED USE → WHAT WE KNOW → WHAT WE DON'T KNOW → TEST / VERIFY → REQUIRED TREATMENT JOBS

2. Clear Water ≠ Safe Water

Appearance is an observation. Safety is a claim that requires evidence matched to the decision.

What your eyes can tell you

Color, visible particles, cloudiness, debris and some obvious physical changes.

What your eyes cannot prove

Absence of disease-causing microorganisms, dissolved chemicals, metals, salts or other invisible hazards.

DIRTY IS VISIBLE. DANGER ISN'T. Diagnose before prescribing.

3. What Should I Test?

Match the testing path to the question and the stakes.

STEP 1

SCREEN IT

Use observations and appropriate screening to identify what may need a closer look. Screening does not automatically equal a final safety decision.

STEP 2

TARGET IT

When source history, local conditions, symptoms, events or prior results point to a specific concern, choose testing that addresses that concern.

STEP 3

KNOW IT

When the decision affects potable-water safety or another high-stakes use, use evidence and testing appropriate to that decision, including certified-lab testing where warranted.

The test method must match the decision stakes. A cheap or convenient test is not automatically the right test.

5. UV: One Treatment Function, Not General Purification

UV belongs under microbial disinfection/inactivation. It must be selected and operated as part of a treatment train.

1. TEST / DIAGNOSEKnow the source and problem.
2. PRETREAT / CLARIFYMeet the reactor's water-quality and clarity requirements.
3. VALIDATED UVUse a certified/validated reactor appropriate to the identified job and certified flow.
4. SAFE DISTRIBUTIONPrevent downstream recontamination.
5. VERIFY / MAINTAINPower, lamp output, fouling, alarms and model-specific service matter.
UV DOES NOT REMOVE: sediment, chemicals, dissolved metals or salts. UV leaves no residual disinfectant in downstream storage/distribution. For safety-critical potable use, the reactor is WORTH BUYING / DO NOT IMPROVISE.

6. UV Claims and Placement Matter

“UV” or “NSF certified” alone is not enough information. The certified claim and system placement must match the job.

NSF/ANSI 55 Class ANSF/ANSI 55 Class B
Designed around a stronger microbiological-disinfection claim for contaminated water within the standard's defined conditions. The exact certified model, configuration and operating limits still control.Designed for supplemental treatment of already disinfected public drinking water or other water the manufacturer/certification defines for that use. It is not interchangeable with a Class A claim.
POU — Point of UseTreats water at a specific use point, such as one faucet or dedicated outlet. Lower scope does not eliminate the need to match flow, water quality and certified claim.
POE — Point of EntryTreats water entering a building/system. Whole-system flow, pretreatment, distribution hygiene, maintenance and failure consequences become larger design considerations.
CLASS + CERTIFIED MODEL + FLOW + WATER CONDITION + PLACEMENT + MAINTENANCE = the real UV decision.
