# Research Foundation — Know Your Water — Home & Well Testing

Project: RRA-2026-0002
Research stage: Initial foundation
Source set status: Agent-vetted; owner overrides applied where present.

> Authority does not replace evidence. Proximity to the source, transparency, corroboration, and relevance matter more than institutional prestige.

> Teach transferable principles, not isolated facts.

## Research question
How should a household decide what to test in private-well or home water, when to test it, how to collect a useful sample, and what a result can and cannot tell them before choosing treatment?

## Research conclusion
The strongest RRA teaching model is not a universal test list. It is a diagnostic sequence:

`SOURCE + INTENDED USE + LOCAL RISK + RECENT CHANGE/EVENT + HOUSEHOLD VULNERABILITY → TEST PLAN → CERTIFIED/APPROPRIATE TEST → RESULT INTERPRETATION → CONFIRMATION WHEN NEEDED → TREATMENT JOB OR SOURCE/WELL CORRECTION → RETEST/VERIFY`

This is a high-confidence conclusion because current EPA and Florida Department of Health guidance agree on routine private-well testing and on expanding testing according to local risk and circumstances.

## 1. Private-well responsibility is different from public-water responsibility
EPA states that private domestic wells are not regulated under the federal Safe Drinking Water Act and that the well owner is responsible for ensuring household drinking-water safety. Florida likewise states that private-well owners are responsible for ensuring their own water is safe to drink.

Teaching implication: a private-well household cannot assume that routine public-system monitoring is happening on its behalf.

## 2. Routine testing creates a baseline
EPA recommends annual testing of private wells for total coliform bacteria, nitrate, total dissolved solids, and pH, with additional testing when other contaminants are suspected. Florida Department of Health recommends at least annual bacteria and nitrate testing for private wells; its current private-well guidance also states that most homeowners should test for lead every three years.

RRA should not flatten these into one universal national rule. The useful distinction is:
- federal EPA general guidance provides a broad baseline;
- state/local health guidance may narrow or expand the practical panel;
- local geology, land use, well condition, household vulnerability and known contamination determine targeted additions.

## 3. Test the question, not the entire periodic table
Florida identifies common risk pathways including septic systems, livestock areas, fertilizers, leaking fuel tanks, pesticide use, landfills and improper disposal of hazardous wastes. Florida also notes that local health departments may recommend additional testing based on land use or naturally occurring contaminants.

EPA similarly advises testing for other contaminants when they are suspected and consulting local health officials about substances common in local groundwater.

Teaching implication: targeted testing begins with a source/risk inventory. A nearby farm, septic failure, flood, fuel spill, unusual geology or known contamination area changes what is worth testing.

## 4. Events and changes can trigger immediate testing
EPA advises immediate testing when there are known groundwater/drinking-water problems in the area and provides problem-to-test examples for changes such as cloudiness, corrosion or treatment-equipment issues. EPA guidance for infants also emphasizes testing after flooding. Historical CDC/EPA material supports additional testing after repairs, changes in taste/odor/appearance and other well-system changes.

RRA should teach event-triggered testing as a separate category from routine annual testing.

Useful trigger categories:
- flooding or inundation;
- well, pump, casing, storage or plumbing repair;
- sudden taste, odor, color or clarity change;
- recurrent gastrointestinal illness in the household;
- known local spill/contamination event;
- new infant/pregnancy or another more vulnerable household situation;
- treatment-system performance question.

## 5. Sample quality matters
Florida BPHL's current bacteriological sampling instructions require an official collection kit, running water before sampling, avoiding hand/finger contact with the stream, collecting the specified volume, same-day delivery and cold transport. EPA likewise notes that laboratories generally provide the proper sample containers and handling instructions and that coliform samples require sterile containers and sterile technique.

Teaching implication: "test accuracy" begins before the laboratory. Wrong bottles, poor handling, bad timing or contamination during collection can make a result less useful.

RRA must direct learners to follow the selected laboratory's exact collection/preservation instructions rather than invent one universal sampling procedure for all analytes.

## 6. Certified laboratories are the decision-grade path
EPA recommends certified drinking-water laboratories for private-well analysis. Florida directs well owners to state-certified/NELAP-certified laboratories and provides county/state laboratory pathways.

At-home screening may be educational or useful for some limited questions, but RRA should not present an unverified consumer kit as equivalent to a certified laboratory when a health/safety decision depends on the number.

Research boundary: this cycle does not yet rank or endorse consumer test kits. Product Design/market research may later compare kit claims, analytes, detection limits and certifications where appropriate.

## 7. Results are measurements, not complete diagnoses
EPA notes that laboratory results report concentrations and may compare them with drinking-water standards. If a contaminant exceeds a health standard, EPA advises contacting public health officials and retesting to confirm presence/concentration before choosing corrective steps.

A result must be interpreted with:
- units;
- analyte;
- detection/reporting limit;
- applicable health or aesthetic benchmark;
- sample location;
- whether treatment was bypassed or included;
- whether confirmation is needed;
- local/state guidance.

RRA should separate three questions:
1. What was detected?
2. Does the level matter for the intended use?
3. What action follows: confirm, inspect source/well, change source, treat, maintain, or monitor?

## 8. A contaminant-specific treatment job comes after testing
EPA states that treatment options depend on the contaminant, concentration and well condition, and that some situations may require a new water source or a new well rather than an in-home treatment device.

This preserves the parent Water project's core principle: diagnosis before prescription.

## 9. Nitrate illustrates why context and vulnerable users matter
EPA's drinking-water MCL for nitrate is 10 mg/L. Florida specifically warns about nitrate risk for infants and recommends annual nitrate testing for private wells. EPA's current infant/private-well guidance states not to use water above 10 mg/L nitrate for infant formula or food.

RRA should teach nitrate as a model of why "clear water" is not the same as "safe water" and why household circumstances can change urgency.

## 10. Florida-specific practicality
For RRA's Florida base, current Florida Department of Health information provides a useful real-world path:
- annual bacteria and nitrate testing is strongly recommended;
- state/county public-health laboratories and certified private labs are available;
- local health departments can advise on additional contaminants;
- Florida aquifer risks vary with septic systems, agriculture, fuel/pesticide sources, landfills and local geology;
- private-home well sampling generally is not a statewide routine legal requirement, so owner responsibility is central.

This project should teach the national principle while using Florida as a detailed case study rather than pretending one state's recommendations are universal.

# TRANSFER THE PRINCIPLE
The transferable principle is **measure the condition that determines the decision before buying the solution**.

Water: test before choosing treatment.
Business: measure the bottleneck before buying software.
Money: find the actual spending pattern before cutting random expenses.
Health/safety systems: identify the hazard before choosing protective equipment.
Land/soil: test the soil problem before adding amendments.

The comparison stops where measurement methods, stakes and professional requirements differ. A water laboratory result, business metric and soil test are not interchangeable forms of evidence; the transferable principle is the diagnostic sequence.

## Evidence boundaries / unresolved questions for later stages
- This research foundation does not yet establish a universal consumer-home-test-kit recommendation.
- It does not yet build a nationwide state-by-state contaminant matrix.
- PFAS, radionuclides, arsenic, pesticides/VOCs and other targeted panels require location/risk-specific research before learner-facing recommendation logic becomes detailed.
- Exact result-interpretation thresholds must be tied to the analyte, jurisdiction, intended use and current source.
- Product Design must avoid duplicating the parent Water product's broader treatment architecture.

## Research recommendation
Approve this foundation for Product Opportunity Research. The strongest differentiated RRA opportunity is a decision tool/learning resource that helps a learner answer **"What should I test, and why?"** before spending money on a broad panel, test kit or treatment device.
