# Think It Through

## Start here: what are you actually looking at?

Before deciding whether you agree, separate the statement.

A real-life sentence can contain several things at once:

- **what happened** — a factual claim that can be checked;
- **what it might mean** — an interpretation or inference;
- **what somebody prefers or thinks should happen** — an opinion/value judgment;
- **what somebody thinks will happen next** — a prediction;
- **what somebody thinks you should do** — a recommendation.

A factual claim can still be wrong. An opinion can still be informed by strong evidence. Your first job is not to pick a side. Your first job is to identify the layers.

## Two labels, not one

Every important statement gets two separate questions:

1. **What kind of claim is this?**
2. **How strong is the evidence?**

Do not let the word “fact” do work the evidence has not earned.

## SORT

Break mixed claims apart.

Example:

> “Sales are down because customers hate our new prices, so we need to lower them.”

Possible layers:

- Sales are down. — factual claim if the records show it.
- Customers hate the new prices. — interpretation/hypothesis unless measured.
- Price is the cause. — causal inference.
- We need to lower prices. — recommendation.

Those layers should not all receive the same evidence label.

**Your move:** split the statement you are examining into every layer that matters.

## SOURCE

Ask where each factual or inferential layer came from.

- What is the closest available evidence?
- Did this source observe, measure or record the thing directly?
- Is this source repeating somebody else?
- Are five posts actually five sources, or five copies of one source?
- What evidence points the other way?
- What is still unknown?

> **Count independent evidence, not echoes.**

Popularity does not replace evidence. Authority does not replace evidence either. Proximity to the source, transparency, corroboration and relevance matter more than institutional prestige.

## Repetition can feel like proof

A claim can become familiar long before it becomes verified. Seeing the same statement repeatedly can make it feel more believable. That is one reason source tracing matters.

Your source map should show roots, not just the number of times a sentence appeared.

## STRESS TEST

Now make your own conclusion work for its confidence.

Ask:

- What would strengthen this conclusion?
- What would weaken it?
- What evidence would make me reverse it?
- What alternative explanation fits the same facts?
- What evidence have I not looked for?
- Would I accept this same quality of evidence if it supported the opposite conclusion?

If nothing could change your mind, you are no longer describing an evidence-responsive conclusion.

## Bias is not only something other people have

People commonly notice bias more easily in others than in themselves. That makes “they are biased” a weak stopping point.

Look both directions:

**Outward:** What incentives, framing, assumptions, identity or evidence limits may affect the other source?

**Inward:** What do I want to be true? What do I already believe? What would embarrass me to admit? Which evidence standard am I using for my own side?

Identifying a possible bias does not prove the person's conclusion is false.

## The room around your decision matters

Your judgment is not made in a vacuum.

Groups can influence decisions in at least four ways:

1. **Information:** “They may know something I don't.”
2. **Belonging:** “I don't want to be the odd one out.”
3. **Norms:** “This is what people like us do or approve of.”
4. **Value weighting:** “Watching what they choose changes what feels important to me.”

Sometimes a person changes what they say publicly without changing what they believe privately. Sometimes social information genuinely improves a decision. Sometimes it pushes judgment away from evidence.

Your job is not to reject the group. Your job is to notice what role the group is playing.

## DECIDE

Confidence should match evidence.

Useful states:

- **Strongly corroborated** — multiple relevant independent lines agree.
- **Supported** — evidence points one way but meaningful uncertainty remains.
- **Reasonable inference** — plausible explanation with some support; alternatives remain.
- **Tentative** — possible but not adequately established.
- **Value judgment** — evidence can inform the decision, but evidence alone does not settle the value question.
- **Unsupported assertion** — adequate support has not been shown.

Then decide what action fits the confidence and the cost of being wrong.

Possible next moves:

- act;
- share;
- wait;
- verify more;
- ask the original source;
- compare another independent source;
- test or measure;
- revise the conclusion.

## My Decision Record

Your final record should answer:

- What claim am I examining?
- What are the separate layers?
- What evidence do I actually have?
- Which sources are independent?
- What is still assumption?
- What would change my mind?
- What social pressure may be affecting the decision?
- What is my current confidence?
- What could happen if I am wrong?
- What am I doing next?
- What remains unresolved?

A Decision Record is living. New evidence should be able to change it.

## Practice: Trace the Echo

Take several posts that repeat the same claim. Trace each one backward.

Ask:

- How many independent sources were there really?
- Did any copy add new evidence?
- Did wording become stronger as the claim spread?
- Did familiarity make the claim feel settled before verification?

## Practice: Bias Mirror

Choose a low-stakes disagreement.

Write:

- pressure/bias I can see on the other side;
- pressure/bias that could affect my side.

The exercise is incomplete until both sides are filled.

## Practice: The Unanimous Room

Use a harmless, objectively checkable task. Record your answer privately before seeing a unanimous group answer. Compare what happens to your public answer and confidence. Then repeat with one dissenting answer present.

The point is not humiliation. The point is to observe how the room can become part of the decision.

## TRANSFER THE PRINCIPLE

> **Separate what happened from what you think it means, separate what you know from what you assume, and make confidence earn its way upward through evidence.**

Use the same method in:

- family disagreements;
- purchases and money decisions;
- business metrics and hypotheses;
- leadership decisions;
- school and research;
- social media and news;
- community claims.

**Where the transfer stops:** evidence can clarify facts and likely consequences, but it does not fully settle genuine differences in values, priorities, morality or acceptable tradeoffs.

Think Like a Rebel means understanding before reacting, questioning assumptions on your own side too, and testing what would change the answer.
