# RRA-2026-0001 — Targeted UV Evidence Addendum

**Status:** READY FOR OWNER EVIDENCE REVIEW  
**Scope:** Ultraviolet microbiological water treatment/disinfection only  
**Relationship to Revision 3:** ADDITIVE ONLY. This does not reopen, replace, weaken, or remove any approved Revision 3 material.

## Research question

Where does ultraviolet treatment belong in the Water Through the Layers decision system, what treatment job can it perform, what conditions determine whether it works, and what limits must remain visible?

## Evidence-backed findings

### 1. UV's treatment job is microbiological disinfection, not general purification

Residential UV systems use ultraviolet light to disinfect water or reduce microorganisms. CDC describes UV treatment systems with pre-filtration as effective against parasites, bacteria, and viruses, while explicitly stating that they do **not** remove chemicals. NSF/ANSI 55 is the residential standard specifically addressing reduction of microorganisms using UV radiation.

**RRA teaching boundary:** UV belongs under the function **DISINFECT / INACTIVATE MICROORGANISMS**. It is not a substitute for sediment removal, adsorption, ion removal, dissolved-chemical treatment, desalination, or contaminant-specific filtration.

### 2. Diagnose before prescribing still controls the decision

EPA's current private-well guidance says treatment should follow testing: if a contaminant exceeds a health standard, confirm the result and choose treatment appropriate to the contaminant and concentration. EPA lists UV among possible disinfection methods for high bacteria findings, not as a universal well-water treatment.

**RRA teaching boundary:** `well water + unknown quality` does not equal `install UV`. The correct path is **test → identify problem → assign treatment job → select validated method → verify**.

### 3. Water clarity and pre-filtration matter

CDC states that UV systems that filter water before UV treatment work better than systems without pre-filtration. CDC's emergency guidance is more explicit: UV does not work well in cloudy water because particles can block microorganisms from the light.

**RRA functional translation:** sediment/turbidity control may be a prerequisite function because UV must reach the microorganisms to inactivate them. A learner should not treat UV as a magic final step placed behind visibly dirty water.

### 4. NSF/ANSI 55 Class A and Class B are different claims

NSF's consumer standards overview distinguishes the classes:

- **Class A:** systems are intended to inactivate or kill bacteria, viruses, and cysts in contaminated water.
- **Class B:** systems are intended to reduce non-disease-causing bacteria in drinking water that is already disinfected.

Current NSF listings show both Class A and Class B systems across plumbed-in and point-of-entry products, and list certified flow rates for individual models.

**RRA teaching boundary:** `NSF/ANSI 55 certified` is incomplete unless the learner checks the **class, claim, configuration, and certified flow rate**.

### 5. Flow rate is part of performance

Current NSF certified listings pair specific UV models with specific rated flow rates and Class A or Class B disinfection claims. That means a device cannot responsibly be treated as equally effective at any flow rate.

**RRA functional translation:** UV performance depends on delivering the validated exposure while water moves through the reactor. A build planner should ask for required household flow and should not encourage bypassing a certified device's rated operating conditions.

### 6. UV does not remove dissolved chemicals, metals, salts, or sediment

CDC states UV treatment with pre-filtration does not remove chemicals. This is the critical reason UV must be one branch in a treatment train rather than the definition of "purification."

Examples of separate treatment jobs may include:

- sediment/turbidity reduction;
- microbial disinfection;
- activated-carbon adsorption for appropriate compounds/taste/odor functions;
- ion/mineral treatment;
- reverse osmosis or other contaminant-specific technologies where evidence and test results support them.

### 7. UV normally sits after the water has been made suitable for UV exposure

The functional sequence for a UV branch is generally:

`SOURCE → TEST/DIAGNOSE → PRETREAT AS REQUIRED → REDUCE TURBIDITY / INTERFERING MATERIAL AS REQUIRED → VALIDATED UV DISINFECTION → SAFE STORAGE/DISTRIBUTION → VERIFY / MAINTAIN`

This is a teaching architecture, not a universal plumbing prescription. Actual treatment train design must respond to the tested water and the certified system's instructions.

### 8. Point-of-use and point-of-entry solve different delivery problems

CDC defines point-of-use systems as treating water delivered to a single tap and point-of-entry/whole-home systems as treating most or all water entering the home. Current NSF listings include countertop/plumbed-in units and point-of-entry units under NSF/ANSI 55.

**RRA scenario logic:**

- Countertop/single-tap drinking-water goals may point to a POU path.
- Whole-house microbial treatment may point to POE.
- The correct choice also depends on test results, required flow, other treatment jobs, plumbing, power, maintenance, and whether untreated water remains elsewhere in the system.

### 9. UV creates no residual disinfectant protection downstream

UV treats water as it passes through the irradiation zone; unlike a residual chemical disinfectant, it does not leave an ongoing disinfectant concentration in downstream storage or plumbing. This matters because post-treatment contamination and poorly maintained downstream components can reintroduce microbial risk.

**RRA teaching boundary:** disinfection is not the same as permanent protection. Storage, plumbing hygiene, maintenance, and recontamination control remain system functions.

### 10. Maintenance is part of the treatment function

A certified device's lamp/LED source, reactor, sleeve or optical path, sensors/alarms when present, flow conditions, power supply, and manufacturer maintenance schedule are part of the validated system. Learner-facing instructions should always route specific lamp replacement, sleeve cleaning, alarm response, and service intervals to the exact certified model's current manufacturer instructions rather than inventing one universal schedule.

**RRA teaching rule:** if a safety-critical device depends on maintenance to deliver its claimed treatment, maintenance is not an accessory topic; it is part of the treatment system.

### 11. Failure modes / false-security risks

Important failure modes include:

- cloudy/turbid water blocking UV exposure;
- excessive flow beyond validated conditions;
- power loss;
- degraded light output or overdue maintenance;
- fouling/scaling or dirty optical surfaces;
- using Class B where Class A performance is actually needed;
- assuming microbial disinfection also removed chemical contaminants;
- installing treatment before testing and therefore solving the wrong problem;
- recontamination after treatment or in storage/distribution;
- relying on a non-validated homemade UV reactor for potable-water safety claims.

### 12. DIY boundary: teach the system, do not improvise the safety-critical reactor

RRA can safely teach and diagram:

- treatment-job diagnosis;
- pre-filtration concepts;
- gravity versus pumped flow;
- storage and distribution functions;
- POU versus POE architecture;
- how flow affects exposure;
- where a certified UV module belongs;
- power/backup implications;
- maintenance and verification logic.

For potable-water microbial disinfection, RRA should recommend a **validated/certified purchased UV treatment device appropriate to the identified job** rather than provide instructions for a homemade UV reactor whose microbial performance has not been validated.

This is exactly where `function before form` has a safety boundary: many objects can perform the broad function "shine UV light," but they are not automatically equivalent in dose, wavelength, hydraulic behavior, monitoring, reliability, or verified pathogen reduction.

### 13. Low-resource alternatives for the same disinfection function

CDC documents several household methods that can perform the **microbial-disinfection function** when UV equipment/electricity is not appropriate or available:

- boiling;
- chlorine-based disinfection;
- solar disinfection using transparent containers and sunlight under specified conditions;
- combined filtration plus chemical disinfection for emergency use.

These alternatives are not interchangeable in every scenario. They differ in fuel/electricity needs, time, residual protection, organism performance, chemical-contaminant limitations, taste, consumables, operator discipline, volume, and reliability.

**Think Like a Rebel transfer:** do not ask "What replaces a UV machine?" Ask "What function am I trying to perform, under what conditions, with what resources, and how will I verify the outcome?"

## Direct answers to Product Design's 12 questions

1. **Job:** microbiological disinfection/inactivation.
2. **Microorganisms:** validated systems can address parasites/cysts, bacteria, and viruses according to their certified claim; performance depends on system class and operating conditions.
3. **Class A vs B:** Class A is for contaminated/microbiologically unsafe water claims; Class B reduces non-disease-causing bacteria in already disinfected water.
4. **Pretreatment:** clarity/turbidity matters; pre-filtration improves UV treatment performance; the exact pretreatment train depends on tested water and device instructions.
5. **Does not remove:** chemicals, dissolved metals/salts, and sediment simply by UV microbial treatment.
6. **Treatment train position:** after diagnosis and required pretreatment/clarification; before safe downstream storage/distribution.
7. **Power/maintenance:** power, validated flow, light-source condition, optical cleanliness, alarms/monitoring and model-specific service are part of dependable performance.
8. **Failure risks:** opacity/turbidity, excess flow, power failure, fouling, degraded lamp/output, wrong class, recontamination, and false assumptions about chemical removal.
9. **POU vs POE:** single-tap versus whole-home delivery function; select based on scope, tested need, flow and system architecture.
10. **Private wells:** test first; UV may be appropriate for a confirmed microbial problem as part of a matched treatment plan; re-test/verify as appropriate.
11. **Educational vs build:** teach/DIY the surrounding system logic; use validated/certified equipment for the safety-critical potable-water UV disinfection reactor.
12. **Low-resource alternatives:** boiling, chemical disinfection, solar disinfection and layered emergency treatment can perform related microbial-control functions with different tradeoffs and limitations.

# TRANSFER THE PRINCIPLE

## Function before form

A "UV purifier" is a **form**. The underlying function is **microbial disinfection**.

Once the learner sees the function, the decision becomes broader and more intelligent:

- What organism risk am I solving?
- What prevents the selected method from working?
- What resources do I have?
- What other methods can perform the same function?
- Which substitutions are safe and validated for this use?
- What happens downstream?
- How do I know the treatment is still working?

This same thinking transfers outside water. A product name is not a problem definition. Break the system down to its required functions, constraints, failure modes and verification method before deciding what to buy or build.

## Evidence boundary

This addendum supports learner-facing explanation of UV as a treatment branch. It does **not** authorize claims that any UV lamp, LED, wand, homemade reactor, or uncertified device makes unknown water potable. It does not authorize bypassing testing, certified-device operating limits, manufacturer maintenance instructions, or contaminant-specific treatment needs.

## Research conclusion

**PASS — targeted evidence gap is filled sufficiently for Product Design to integrate UV into the treatment-job decision system, provided the boundaries above remain visible.**
