# RRA-2026-0004 — Product Manuscript

**Working learner title:** Build My Water Storage System  
**Research title:** Containers Are Containers — Safe Storage by Function

## Opening

A container that holds water is not automatically the right container for every water job.

A jug, barrel, tote, tank, cistern and trough are all forms. The first question is not **Which one should I buy?**

The first question is:

> **What does my storage system actually need to do?**

You may need drinking water, emergency backup, animal water and irrigation at the same time. Those jobs can share some equipment and still need different rules.

This product helps you inspect what you already have, decide what each container can responsibly do, see what needs fixing, calculate capacity and weight, and build one clear plan without pretending every container is interchangeable.

---

# 1. Start With the Job

Choose every use that applies to you:

- Drinking or cooking
- Emergency household reserve
- Livestock or animals
- Garden / ordinary irrigation
- Produce-contact or postharvest agricultural use
- Another non-potable job

You do **not** have to choose only one.

### Plain truth
The consequence of contamination changes with the job.

Water stored only for ordinary non-food irrigation is not the same job as water stored for drinking. Animal water has its own maintenance needs. Water that contacts produce after harvest can require stronger controls than ordinary irrigation.

### Tell me more
The useful question is not “Is this a water container?” It is “What functions and consequences come with this use?”

---

# 2. Use What You Have

Before buying another barrel or tank, look at what you already own.

For each container, record:
- what it is;
- how much it holds;
- what it held before;
- what you know about the material;
- its condition;
- how it closes;
- how water comes out;
- whether you can clean and inspect it;
- where it will sit;
- what supports it.

Availability is not the same as suitability. The goal is to find what can responsibly perform the job — and what cannot.

---

# 3. The History Gate

## What did this container hold before?

### Known drinking-water or food use
Continue the inspection. Prior use alone does not finish the decision, but it gives you useful information.

### Unknown history
Do not guess that it is suitable for drinking water. Identify what can be verified. If the history cannot be established, a known-suitable potable container may be the simpler choice for the drinking branch.

### Toxic chemicals, pesticides or petroleum products
**Do not use that vessel for potable-water storage.** Home cleaning does not make this an approved drinking-water reuse path.

This is one of the places where “use what you have” stops. Resourcefulness still has boundaries.

---

# 4. See What Makes a Storage System Work

A storage container does more than hold liquid.

Look at these parts and ask what each one does:

### Lid / closure
Keeps debris, hands, insects, animals and other contamination from entering.

### Fill opening
Large enough to fill and service the container, but protected enough that routine access does not become contamination access.

### Spigot / outlet / valve
Lets water leave without dipping cups or hands into the stored supply.

### Cleaning access
If you cannot inspect or clean the inside when the system requires it, that matters.

### Base / support
Water is heavy. A large full container changes the load on floors, shelves, stands and platforms.

### Location
Direct sun, heat, chemicals, fuel, manure, feed and other environmental conditions can change the storage problem.

### Label / date
A container that becomes forgotten inventory is not a maintained system.

# 5. What Each Water Job Needs

## Drinking / cooking
Focus on:
- known suitable prior use/context;
- cleanable protected storage;
- a covered opening;
- dispensing without routine dipping;
- protected placement;
- maintenance and labeling.

## Emergency household reserve
Add:
- enough capacity for your chosen planning horizon;
- access during an outage;
- portability vs bulk-storage tradeoffs;
- fill/rotation records;
- separation from chemicals and fuels;
- redundancy: one large container is different from several smaller ones.

## Livestock / animals
Focus on:
- enough capacity and refill reliability;
- cleanable trough/tank surfaces;
- manure, feed, dirt and algae control;
- safe animal access;
- recurring inspection/cleaning;
- testing when a real quality concern changes the decision.

## Garden / ordinary irrigation
Focus on:
- capacity;
- delivery compatibility;
- placement and overflow;
- maintenance appropriate to the actual job.

## Produce-contact / postharvest
Do not treat this as ordinary irrigation by default. The exact crop, contact and use can materially change the requirement. This product keeps the branch visible and tells you when more specific evidence is needed rather than inventing a universal rule.

# 6. Capacity, Weight & Redundancy

More gallons are not automatically better if the system becomes impossible to move, support, clean or access.

### Water weight planning
Approximate planning weight:

> **gallons × 8.34 lb = water weight**

This is the water alone, before the container, frame, fittings or platform are added.

### One large container
Possible advantages:
- efficient use of footprint;
- fewer separate vessels to maintain;
- high capacity in one place.

Tradeoffs:
- heavy when full;
- harder to move;
- may be harder to clean/service;
- can become a single point of failure.

### Several smaller containers
Possible advantages:
- easier handling;
- easier rotation;
- redundancy if one container leaks or becomes questionable.

Tradeoffs:
- more lids/fittings to maintain;
- more storage pieces;
- potentially more floor/shelf complexity.

The right answer follows your job and constraints.

---

# 7. Fix What Matters First

Do not start with optional upgrades.

Correct in this order:

1. **Hard disqualifier** — wrong prior contents for the intended job.
2. **Structural problem** — unstable support, leak or failure risk.
3. **Contamination path** — open/dipping access, poor closure, dirty dispensing path.
4. **Capacity gap** — not enough storage for the selected job.
5. **Maintenance gap** — no realistic way to inspect, clean, record or rotate.
6. **Convenience upgrade** — level indicators, automation, prettier fittings, etc.

---

# 8. Storage Is an Operating System

Buying or filling a container is not the finish line.

Use this loop:

> **Fill → Label → Inspect → Clean → Maintain / Rotate → Verify after a real concern → Correct**

The exact schedule depends on the system and use. The important part is that maintenance is designed in rather than remembered only after something looks wrong.

---

# 9. Does Function Before Form Hold Water?

Choose one real container you own.

### UNDERSTAND
What job are you assigning it?

### INSPECT
Check history, condition, closure, dispensing, cleaning access, location and support.

### USE / OBSERVE
What actually happens during filling, dispensing and normal use?

### CHECK FAILURE POINTS
What happens if the lid is left open? The spigot gets dirty? The tank sits near fuel? A trough fills with feed/manure? One large tank fails?

### IMPROVE
Make one responsible correction.

### DECIDE
Does this form still fit the job?

### TRANSFER THE PRINCIPLE
Where else do you own something because of its label when what really matters is the function it performs?

A successful storage setup is not proof of water quality when a quality question requires testing. Test or verify the thing that actually changes the decision.

---

# 10. My Water Storage Plan

Your final plan should show:
- every water job you selected;
- every container you inventoried;
- what each container can do by branch;
- which containers should not be used for a selected job and why;
- which unknowns need verification;
- your planned capacity and approximate water weight;
- shared infrastructure;
- branch-specific corrections;
- your maintenance/inspection routine;
- optional upgrades;
- next learning steps.

This is a living plan. If you change a use, add a container, learn its prior history, or change capacity, the dependent plan should update.

# TRANSFER THE PRINCIPLE

**Requirements follow the job, not the label on the object.**

Use that thinking in food storage, fuel, tools, inventory, emergency reserves and business systems: define the job and failure consequence first, inventory what you have, identify the required functions, then decide whether the form is actually suitable.

Do not stretch the analogy past the evidence. Water-specific contamination and material-contact risks remain specific to water.
